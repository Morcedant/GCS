                  Gothic horror in the Grimm-verse

                  Grimm Players' Guide

                  The late 1800s

                  Every fairy tale was a warning. The Brothers Grimm wrote down what their family had hunted for centuries, and the things in those stories never went away. In fog-bound towns and lamplit camps, Wesen walk among the living wearing human faces. Grimms, the last of the old hunters, can see through them. The Royal Families pull strings from across the ocean, and men and women with real magic hide inside lodges and country churches. You know the truth, and you stand between it and the people who don’t.

                  This guide uses the same rules as the USS Pelion game: concrete skills, 3d6 roll-under, and a small pool of Grit points. The material for running the game (case seeds, the threats, and the GM’s tools) is in a companion book, the GM’s Guide. It is an unofficial fan game inspired by the television series Grimm and is not affiliated with its makers.

                  The rule to remember

                  Roll 3d6 at or below your adjusted skill to succeed. Your skill already includes your training and attribute. Do not add the attribute again.

                  Example: Tracking 13, with a −2 penalty for rain, gives a target of 11. A roll of 9 succeeds by 2. A roll of 12 fails by 1.

                  The Grimm-verse

                  Wesen look like ordinary people but have a hidden form, almost always a two-legged blend of human and animal. The change between the two is the woge (VOH-gə). Each kind of Wesen has its own culture and traditions, and many of the oldest involve hunting or eating humans. The Blutbad, a wolf-like Wesen, gave rise to the werewolf legend. Their names are German, as are many of the old words of this hidden world.

                  Grimms are supernaturally gifted warriors, stronger and faster than ordinary people, who can see the Wesen hidden among us. Their old Latin name, Decapitare, comes from their habit of beheading the creatures they hunt. The gift passes only occasionally, sometimes skipping a generation or two, and the Grimm families keep themselves anonymous. Grimms enforce the Code of Swabia, the old law between Wesen and the rest of the world. Some Grimms enforce it fairly; others believe every Wesen is an abomination to be destroyed.

                  The seven Royal Families are ancient, wealthy and influential. They no longer rule whole countries, but they still manipulate events through their public cousins, field loyal Wesen armies, and seek control over all Wesen and over ordinary humanity too.

                  Incantare is the catch-all name for anyone who works real magic. Most magic is ritual, in many styles and beliefs, and each practitioner sees it through a Lens. The Wesen Council and the Royal Families both consider Incantare extremely dangerous, and Grimms are often tasked with watching or destroying them. The blood of a Grimm is a powerful anti-magic agent. A Hexenbiest or Zauberbiest who drinks it under a full moon loses their powers, and a Chymiater can brew it into a draught that strips anyone’s magical gifts for days (see Grimm blood).

                  Three orders matter on the frontier:

                  • Bruderschaft der Heiligen Erde (Brotherhood of the Holy Earth) protects the Earth from industry. Luddites with magic, they claim descent from druidic orders, and the railroad is their enemy.
                  • Ordo Sancti Cultelli (Order of the Holy Knife) is a Church-affiliated order of assassins sworn to cleanse the Earth of supernatural threats, Wesen and Incantare alike.
                  • Ordo Hermeticus Aurorae Aureae (the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn) presents itself as a fraternal order with odd trappings. Inside it, layer under layer, are secrets and real magicians.

                  Create your character

                  Work through the five steps in order. The Grimm character creator follows the same steps and checks the rules for you.

                  Step 1: Choose your calling

                  Occultists and magic. Magic comes from your heritage, not your calling: an Occultist who is an Incantare works real magic through their Lens. With any other heritage, an Occultist knows the lore but can’t work the art: a scholar of the occult, a charlatan, or someone still searching for a teacher.

                  Every calling has its own ability (in the table above), printed on your sheet. Callings follow one recipe: a fighting skill, a trail skill, a people skill and a signature skill. The Politician and the four scholars and healers of the Learned aren’t fighters: they take a second signature skill instead, though anyone can still train a fighting skill.

                  The creator marks your calling’s four core skills (in the table above) Trained when you choose it. Keep them, raise one to your Specialty, or swap any of them for other skills; changing calling swaps the core skills you haven’t changed.

                  Heritage and tier

                  Choose a heritage from the Heritages chapter: Human, Grimm, Incantare, or one of the Wesen kinds. Every attribute starts at 10, and your heritage adjusts some of them; the result is your heritage base. Adjustments always total zero.

                  Heritages differ in power, so each has a tier from 0 to 3. Every character has 3 points to split between heritage power and Edges: Tier 0 (Human) leaves 3 Edges, Tier 1 leaves 2, Tier 2 leaves 1, and Tier 3 leaves none. Edges are useful in every scene; a Wesen’s power mostly shows while woged, and that risks Exposure.

                  Horror heritages. Naschazer, Taniwha and Vallachi must feed on human flesh or lifeforce. They’re available only when the whole table has agreed to a horror-tinged campaign.

                  Step 3: Choose trained skills

                  A Well-rounded character chooses ten different skills: one Specialty at its governing attribute +3 and nine Trained skills at attribute +1. An Old hand chooses one Expert skill at +5 and seven Trained skills at +1. All other skills are Untrained at attribute −4 when an attempt is plausible. Each skill has its own final target number; do not add the attribute again when rolling.

                  The Expert’s extra bonus matters most when the work is hard. With Intellect 13, an Expert gambler has a target of 18, which only natural 17–18 can fail; under a −4 “very difficult” penalty the target is still 14. If Athletics is the Expert skill, Dodge adds +2, the same as a Specialty.

                  Each Extra Trained skill Edge adds one more Trained skill. A Grimm’s Wesen Lore is always at least Trained and doesn’t use one of the Trained picks.

                  Broadening. An Old hand can broaden later (the GM’s call, usually after a significant case): the Expert skill becomes a Specialty at +3, and two more skills become Trained.

                  Step 4: Describe your character

                  Choose your name, title if any, and a short reason you stand against the dark. Then choose one advantage and one complication.

                  Top 10 reasons to stand against the dark

                  1. The family gift. The Grimm blood woke in me, and it came with a duty.
                  2. Vengeance. Something that wore a human face killed someone I loved.
                  3. Protect the town. Somebody has to stand between these people and what’s out there.
                  4. Keep the peace. Wesen and humans can live side by side, and I mean to prove it.
                  5. I saw it. I saw what I saw, and no one else believes me.
                  6. Redemption. I did harm once. I mean to balance the ledger.
                  7. The bounty. The pay’s good, and nobody else will take these jobs.
                  8. Knowledge. The hidden world is the greatest mystery there is, and I want to understand it.
                  9. Faith. The Lord sent me here for a reason, and I believe this is it.
                  10. Nowhere else to go. These people took me in when no one else would.
                  • Advantage. Choose either a general advantage or one of your heritage advantages. A heritage advantage replaces the general advantage and always brings its companion complication. An Incantare’s heritage advantage is their Lens.
                  • Complication. Choose one complication. You keep it even if you take a heritage advantage, so a character with a heritage advantage has two complications.
                  • Allegiance (optional). Choose an allegiance below, or none.

                  An advantage gives +2 to one narrowly defined kind of task when the GM agrees it applies. It cannot improve every use of a broad skill. A complication creates choices and problems; it is not a permanent penalty.

                  Actions and teamwork

                  When to roll

                  Routine work succeeds automatically when you have appropriate training, gear and time. Roll when failure could change the situation. Before rolling, establish your goal, the relevant skill, any modifiers, and what is at stake.

                  Circumstances Modifier
                  Favorable conditions or excellent preparation +2
                  Ordinary challenge under pressure 0
                  Difficult −2
                  Very difficult −4
                  Extreme −6

                  Apply the main circumstance modifier once. Add separately specified effects such as assistance or injury when they apply. The GM avoids charging twice for the same obstacle.

                  Results and margins

                  Add the three dice. A total at or below the adjusted skill succeeds. Margin of success equals the adjusted skill minus the roll. A larger margin may mean better information, more precise work, or a stronger position.

                  A natural 3 or 4 always succeeds. A natural 17 or 18 always fails. An impossible action receives no roll.

                  In an opposed contest, each side rolls its relevant skill. One success beats one failure. If both succeed, the larger success margin wins. A tie, or two failures, favors the defender or preserves the situation. For an automatic success above its target, count its contest margin as zero.

                  Assistance

                  One qualified assistant can roll a relevant skill before the lead character rolls. Success grants +1 to the lead roll; success by 5 or more grants +2. Failure gives no bonus. An 18 introduces a complication described by the GM. Additional characters can do other tasks but cannot stack assistance bonuses on the same roll.

                  Failure and another attempt

                  Failure changes the situation: time passes, the trail goes cold, supplies run out, or danger grows. Do not repeat an unchanged roll until it succeeds. A new approach, new evidence, better tools, or more time may justify another attempt.

                  Success at a cost

                  When a roll fails by only 1 or 2, the GM may offer success at a cost: you get what you were after, but something else goes wrong. Typical costs are an injury box, a lost horse or broken gun, lost time (tick a clock), a box of Trouble, or someone noticing. The GM names the cost before you accept; you can always take the plain failure instead. A natural 17 or 18 is never a success at a cost.

                  Progress clocks

                  A clock is a circle cut into 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments that tracks something that takes time or builds toward disaster: The mob forms (6), Nightfall (4), Find the lair (6), Win the clan’s trust (8).

                  • Danger clocks fill when things go wrong: a failed roll, a success at a cost, or time passing. When one fills, the danger happens.
                  • Progress clocks fill as the characters succeed: one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. When one fills, the goal is achieved.
                  • The GM sizes the clock (2 = quick, 4 = urgent or simple, 6 = substantial, 8 = long or complex) and shows it to the players.

                  Investigation and social scenes

                  Ask concrete questions and explain your approach. Evidence may require several different tasks. Persuasion can secure cooperation, Read People offers insight into behavior, and Wesen Lore explains what the evidence means. A successful roll does not force someone to abandon a core belief or reveal what they do not know.

                  Chases

                  Run a chase as a progress clock for the pursuers against a danger clock for the quarry’s escape. Each exchange, characters roll Riding (or Athletics on foot): success ticks the pursuers’ clock, failure ticks the escape. Terrain, a tired horse, or a shot fired from the saddle (Shooting at −2) adjust the rolls.

                  • Clearly faster. A side that is a whole class faster (a horse against a runner, a train against a horse) catches up, or gets away, without a roll. Roll the chase between equals, or when terrain or a trick evens things out.
                  • Chases by range. When the distance itself is the fight, run the chase on the battle map’s range track instead of clocks: each exchange, the two sides roll an opposed contest of the skill that moves them, and the winner shifts the range one band, in or out; a tie leaves it. Anyone pushed past Extreme drops out of the chase, and a quarry that wins at Extreme by 5 or more breaks away. The stagecoach chase below is one.
                  • Vehicles take damage on a track. A coach or a wagon has a track of four boxes: one marks no penalty yet; two means a horse or a wheel is hit (−2 to rolls that use it); three, it’s crippled; four, it’s wrecked, and everyone aboard rolls Athletics or marks a box. The stagecoach’s track is below.

                  Stagecoach chases

                  A coach on the road with riders closing in is a chase where distance is everything. Run it on the battle map’s Stagecoach chase track, in the GM view, instead of the chase clocks.

                  • The lanes. The stagecoach has its own lane, and each rider stands in a range band from it: Close (alongside), Short, Medium, Long or Extreme. Riders usually start at Medium or Long.
                  • Each exchange. The driver rolls Riding against each rider’s Riding (11 for a threat without it). The winner moves that rider one band: closer if the rider won, farther if the driver did. A tie leaves it where it is, and a rider pushed past Extreme drops out of the chase. A fresh horse or a good road gives +2; rough ground or a tired team gives −2.
                  • Fighting on the move. Everyone else acts as usual. Shooting from the box, the roof or the saddle is at −2, on top of the range penalty. At Close, a rider can board with an Athletics roll, and then it is fists, knives and shotguns at arm’s length. A passenger can climb out to the box or onto the roof as their move.
                  • Hurting the coach. A hit aimed at the team or a wheel (a called shot, −2) marks one box on the coach’s track. Dynamite, a crash, or a driver who fails by 5 or more on rough ground marks one more.
                  • The driver goes down. If the driver is Incapacitated or knocked from the box, the team bolts. Someone must reach the box and take the reins (Riding −2) before the next exchange, or the coach marks a box.

                  The diaries

                  For generations, Grimms have written down what they learned about the Wesen they hunted, and kept the books in trunks and wagons and hidden rooms. Anyone who investigates the hidden world keeps a diary of their own: what they’ve established for themselves, one finding at a time.

                  Folklore, truth and the diary

                  • Folklore. What people say about the thing in the woods: stories, warnings, a preacher’s sermon, an old Grimm diary’s sketch. Some of it is right, some half right, some wrong. It’s where an investigation starts.
                  • The truth. What a Wesen, a curse or a hex really is, and what really stops it. The GM knows it; you find it out.
                  • Diary entries. What your character has established for themselves: one finding at a time, with its limits, how you know it and what it cost (the core rules call them Legend cards).

                  A real weakness works whether or not anyone holds an entry for it. Act on what you suspect: try silver, salt the threshold, call it by its kind. The GM will ask what your idea rests on, and a good reason is all it needs. Learning, though, is never free.

                  Discovery clocks

                  When a case turns on something unknown (a body torn open on the trail, a town that won’t talk), the GM sets up a discovery clock. It fills in three phases, and filling it earns a diary entry.

                  Phase What it means
                  1. Confront the unknown Effects without a cause. You may suspect a Wesen, but nothing is confirmed.
                  2. Identify Name it: the kind of Wesen, the curse, the hex, the hand behind it. This is the gate: until it’s confirmed, what you know about a kind of Wesen doesn’t yet apply to this one, and your theories stay suspicions.
                  3. Uncover Learn how it is stopped, or what it wants. Filling this phase fills the clock and earns a diary entry for what you established.
                  • Sizing. The GM decides how many segments each phase needs (usually 1–2 to Confront, 2–3 to Identify, 2–3 to Uncover) and shows the clock to the players.
                  • Advancing. Each roll that advances the clock uses a concrete method tied to a skill (Wesen Lore, Investigation, Tracking, Read People, Occult, Apothecary): one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. The clock stops at the end of Identify until the subject is confirmed: segments never spill past the gate.
                  • The primary investigator. One character leads and owns the entry. Others assist under the usual assistance rules. A character who holds a relevant entry can assist with a method they couldn’t otherwise bring; it adds nothing beyond the normal assistance.
                  • Pushing. A player can push to dig faster or harder: a success advances one extra segment, but a box of Trouble is marked whatever the roll.
                  • A head start. A character trained in the skill whose field squarely covers the subject (Wesen Lore before a Wesen, Occult before a hex, Apothecary before a poison) recognizes at once that it’s something new: the clock starts at Identify, skipping Confront. The GM says whether a skill fits.

                  A diary entry

                  An entry grants no bonus to any roll. It’s what the character knows for certain and can rely on, plan around and teach.

                  • The gate. Holding an entry about one kind of Wesen never proves that the one in front of you is that kind: only the Identify phase does.
                  • Shared copies. Everyone who establishes a finding, or is there when the entry is earned, gets a shared copy; the primary investigator is its owner. Someone who wasn’t there can be taught it later, from a reliable account.
                  • What it records. The subject, the precise finding, its limits (what it doesn’t show), the evidence or source, how it was learned, what it cost, the date, its owner and who received it. It’s kept in the creator, under the diary.

                  The threat clock, or a rival

                  A discovery clock races a threat clock: the Wesen killing again, the mob forming, the trail going cold. When it fills, the danger happens. When nothing threatens yet, give the discovery a rival instead: an ordinary danger clock named for someone racing the characters to the same find (a Verrat agent, a Reaper, a newspaperman). It ticks on delays, noisy methods and open digging; when it fills, the rival gets there first.

                  What investigating costs

                  When an investigation roll fails, or a player pushes, one of the Trouble tracks ticks up by one, chosen by how the characters are digging: Suspicion for asking around town and digging in public, Exposure for prying into the Wesen world in the open, Attention for crossing the Royals, the Order or the Reapers. A failed roll can still turn up a lead, but the box is still marked.

                  Learning without a clock

                  A diary entry can also come directly, without a clock. Information is never free: each source has its price, marked when the entry is written.

                  How it was learned What counts What it costs
                  A discovery clock Filled to the end of Uncover. What the clock cost along the way
                  Seen or tested firsthand What you saw yourself, or a careful test: silver on its skin, salt across its path. A box of Trouble, or a tick on the threat clock
                  A reliable account An old Grimm diary, a Wesen elder, a trusted witness. A source’s reputation alone doesn’t make it right. A box of Trouble
                  Its own word, backed up What a Wesen, or someone close to it, tells you, supported by evidence. A box of Trouble

                  Proposing a theory costs nothing; learning something does. The GM provides enough clues, and more than one route to each answer: a case shouldn’t stall on a single missed roll.

                  When the other side investigates you

                  Enemies keep files too. What a Wesen family, the Verrat or a Reaper has learned about a character (a weakness, a habit, a real name, the people they love) gives it +2 against them when it applies: an ambush on the road they always ride, a trap built for a weakness, a hostage who matters. The GM keeps these files and decides when one applies. A character who finds out a file exists (a captured lackey, a stolen ledger) sees it on their sheet, and can destroy it: burn the papers, discredit the source, deal with the spy. A destroyed file gives nothing.

                  The woge and the hidden world

                  Wesen and the woge

                  Every Wesen heritage follows the core Wesen rules and has a woge profile (both in the Heritages chapter). In short:

                  • Woging is a free action, in or out. Physical heritage advantages and the woge profile work only in full woge.
                  • Losing control. When an enemy appears, you’re frightened or you’re attacked, the GM can call for a Will roll; on a failure you woge.
                  • Your profile. While woged, the profile’s attribute bonuses apply to rolls on those attributes and the skills based on them, even above the starting maximum; its other traits and drawbacks apply too. Your sheet has a Human face / Full woge switch: it shows the numbers of the face you’re wearing (the human ones beside them), and the dice roller and the GM’s Encounter panel follow it.
                  • Who sees it. A full woge is visible to anyone. A Grimm sees a flash of the Wesen face whenever a Wesen is stirred by strong emotion. A Wesen who looks into the eyes of a Grimm in that moment knows the Grimm for what they are.
                  • Exposure. A full woge in front of ordinary people breaks the Code of Swabia: mark a box of Exposure (two for a Tier 3 heritage).

                  Grimms

                  A Grimm’s core gifts come with the heritage: the Sight, Grimm eyes, an uneasy sense of nearby Wesen and magic, never freezing (+2 to Will against fear), resisting magic (+2 to Will against it), and Wesen Lore at least Trained for free. Each Grimm also chooses one Grimm gift as their heritage advantage. Grimms enforce the Code, and Wesen know it, so many flee, lie or fight on recognizing one.

                  Grimm blood

                  • Against a Hexenbiest or Zauberbiest. A Hexenbiest or Zauberbiest who drinks a Grimm’s blood under a full moon loses their powers. It happens whether the Grimm intends it or not, and it lasts until the Contaminatio Ritualis restores them.
                  • The Chymiater’s draught. A Chymiater (alchemist) Incantare can brew a Grimm’s fresh blood into a draught in a major ritual (6 segments). Injected or drunk, it neutralizes the target’s magical gifts for 3d6 ÷ 3 days (round down: one to six days): an Incantare’s Lens, a Hexenbiest’s telekinesis and spells, or a Wesen’s magical signature power. It does not stop a woge.
                  • A dangerous commodity. Everyone in the hidden world knows what Grimm blood can do. Who gets it, and how, is always a serious matter.

                  Magic

                  Magic works at two speeds, with a third tool that turns one into the other. An Incantare’s Lens decides which they use and how; any Lens can attempt any effect.

                  Tool Speed How it works
                  Spells One action A quick spell you know. Roll Occult; on a success it happens. Veneficare only.
                  Rituals Minutes to nights Anything else. Describe the effect; the GM sets its weight; fill a progress clock.
                  Charms One action, no roll A finished ritual stored in an object, released by breaking it.

                  Rituals

                  1. Describe the effect. The GM sets its weight: minor (2 segments), significant (4) or major (6), and says what it can and can’t do before you start.
                  2. Gather the energy. Fill a progress clock of that size with Occult rolls. Each roll takes your Lens’s casting time (usually ten minutes; an hour for a Magi). Success fills one segment; success by 5 or more fills two. A failure costs time or ticks a danger clock.
                  3. The effect happens when the clock is full. A ritual interrupted with energy gathered may go wrong.

                  Trappings. The right materials and correspondences (stones, herbs, stars, places) remove one segment from the clock.

                  Charms, elixirs and potions

                  Charms. A finished ritual can be stored in an object and released by breaking it: a bottle thrown, a pendant snapped, a bullet-charm fired. You can carry as many ready charms as your Occult training allows: Trained 2, Specialty 3, Expert 4. A Scaenarfex carries one more; a Veneficare only one. Preparation is how slow magic wins a fast fight: a ritual itself is too slow for one. A charm skips the casting roll, not the target’s chance: it counts as a success by 0, so the target defends with no attack-quality penalty, or resists on a successful roll. A charm never marks more than 3 boxes.

                  Elixirs and potions spoil. They stay good until the end of the day, or a week on a success by 5 or more.

                  Old-country magic in America

                  The old traditions crossed the ocean with the people who kept them, and many of the Lenses wear American clothes:

                  • Braucherei: the Pennsylvania Dutch healing craft of spoken charms, prayers and blessed objects, from Hohman’s The Long Lost Friend (1820). A Braucher heals, protects and breaks hexes, takes no money for it, and passes the craft man to woman and woman to man. It is a Christian craft, and its practitioners would be insulted to be called witches.
                  • Hexerei: Braucherei’s dark mirror. A Hexenmeister lays curses, or is hired to find and break someone else’s, which makes them both feared and needed. The Malifica Lens.
                  • The Himmelsbrief, a “letter from Heaven”: a protective letter carried on the body. Union and Confederate soldiers of German stock carried them against bullets.
                  • Grimoires: the Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses and the Egyptian Secrets of Albertus Magnus pass from hand to hand in German print. A Magi would kill for a good copy; some have.
                  • The Pennsylvania mystics: the Hermits of the Wissahickon and the Ephrata Cloister kept astrology, alchemy and stranger things alive in the last century. A Magi or Chymiater may claim their teacher’s teacher studied there.
                  • The Druid lodges: the United Ancient Order of Druids keeps lodges across the West, California most of all. Most members are only there for the fellowship. Not all.
                  • New arrivals: German colonists from Russia have been settling Kansas and Nebraska since 1874, and brought their own healers and charm-workers with them.

                  Magic draws attention. Using it openly can mark a box of Exposure, the Order of the Holy Knife hunts known Incantare, and a Grimm gets +1 to sense your magic for each charm or potion you carry.

                  The Code of Swabia

                  The Code of Swabia is the old law between Wesen and the rest of the world, and Grimms are its enforcers. At its heart, Wesen must not reveal themselves to humanity. How far it reaches beyond that, and how mercifully a Grimm enforces it, is a question your characters will answer for themselves.

                  Personal combat

                  Turn order and actions

                  Act in descending Agility order. Players choose the order of tied characters; the GM resolves other ties. A round lasts a few seconds. On your turn, move up to your Move (see Move and reach) and take one meaningful action, such as shooting, reloading, helping, or attempting a task.

                  • Acting first and acting twice. An ability can let a character act first in a round (a quick draw): they go before everyone else, in Agility order among themselves. An ability that gives more than one action a round gives each one in turn, before the next character acts.
                  • Helping in a fight. Helping a friend’s attack (drawing a gunman’s eye, steadying a shot) is your action: you assist under the usual rules (+1, or +2 by 5 or more) before they roll.

                  Attacking and defending

                  Roll Shooting for a ranged attack, Melee for a weapon in hand, or Brawling for fists and grappling. If the attack succeeds, an aware target able to move may defend: Dodge, Parry or Block. A successful defense cancels the hit. Apply injury penalties to every defense.

                  • One defense a round. A target gets one defense a round, between its turns, and chooses which when an attack hits.
                  • Additional Defense. With this Edge you may attempt a second defense each round at −2, in addition to attack-quality and injury penalties. You cannot defend twice against the same attack.
                  • Dodge is Agility −2, with +1 for Trained Athletics or +2 for Athletics as a Specialty or Expert.
                  • Parry. Against a weapon in hand, fists, claws or fangs, roll Melee −3 with a weapon in hand. Bare-handed, Brawling −3 parries fists only: nobody turns a Blutbad’s claws with an open hand. Nobody parries a bullet or an arrow.
                  • Block. With a shield, or anything that can stand in for one (a chair, a wooden slat, a crate lid, a saddle), you can block a blade, a club, claws, fangs, fists or an arrow: roll Melee −2, or Melee −1 with a real shield. Nothing you can lift blocks a bullet. An improvised shield breaks the first time it blocks a hit of 2 or more boxes: a chair stops one swipe of a Blutbad’s claws, and then it’s kindling.
                  • Attack quality. A clean hit is harder to escape. When an attack succeeds by 3 or 4, the defense is at −1; by 5 or 6, −2; by 7 or more, −3. A Wesen in full woge often strikes that cleanly.
                  • Aim. Spend your action aiming at a target you can see: +1 to your next attack against it. Careful Marksman and Hunter’s Eye add their +2 to this; a Gunslinger doesn’t need to aim.

                  Solid cover blocks a shot. Partial cover imposes −2 on the attack. Weapon effects apply after a hit survives any defense. Success margin does not increase damage: a great hit decides what happens instead.

                  Telling hits

                  A really good shot, or a blow that lands exactly where it was meant to, lets the attacker say what it does.

                  • A telling hit is an attack that succeeds by 5 or more, or on a natural 3 or 4, and isn’t stopped by a defense. The attacker picks one effect below and describes it. The weapon still marks its usual boxes.
                  • A called shot. Before rolling, name an effect your weapon can deliver and take −2 on the attack, or −4 for a small target (a hand, an eye, a rope, a lock). If the attack hits at all, the effect happens, whatever the margin. Careful Marksman and Hunter’s Eye cut this penalty by 1.
                  • Both ways. Wesen, outlaws and worse land telling hits on characters too.
                  Effect With What happens

                  Knock down A weapon in hand, fists or claws, or a shot or missile that marks 2 or more boxes The target falls prone, or is driven back a few steps. Standing up uses the target’s move on their next turn; they still take their action
                  Pin A weapon in hand, fists, claws or a grapple The target is grabbed, held, or pinned against a wall or the ground.                   Limits. A telling hit never adds boxes and never gets past a defense. It never kills outright, disables a character for good, or destroys exceptional equipment: those come only from injury, from the Dying and death rules, or from an established weakness (a Wesen’s bane, what the Grimm diaries say kills it). Only the effects a weapon can deliver are open to it (the With column). Keeping someone’s head down with gunfire isn’t a pin: that’s cover and a threat. The effect still has to make sense: nobody disarms a Blutbad of its claws or knocks down a stagecoach, and the GM can rule an effect out. Opponents who drop from one hit are already down, so a telling hit against them sets up what comes next.

                  Staying alive. A character on the receiving end can shrug off a telling hit’s effect with Hard to put down (once per fight) or by spending a Grit point. Either way, they still take the boxes.

                  Example: a Blutbad in full woge claws at Deputy Ames and succeeds by 5. Ames hasn’t defended yet this round: Dodge 11, −2 for the clean hit, needs a 9 or less. Ames has a saber in hand, so a parry is possible too: Melee 14 −3 −2 = 9, no better. Ames dodges. When the second Blutbad lunges before Ames’s next turn, there’s no defense left, unless Ames has Additional Defense: a second Dodge at 11 −2 = 9, before any clean-hit penalty.

                  Dying and death

                  • Dying. A hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes and brings you to three boxes leaves you Incapacitated and Dying. A Dying character dies at the end of the scene unless someone stabilizes them: one action and a successful Medicine roll with a doctor’s bag.

                  • Dazed. −2 on your next action roll; then it’s over.

                  Mental harm (an option)

                  A GM whose cases turn on curses and minds broken by the hidden world can give every character a Mind track beside the injury track.                   Weapons and damage

                  How many boxes a hit marks depends on the weapon, not on how well the attack succeeded.

                  • “3, and Dying.” The deadliest weapons (a buffalo rifle, a shotgun at Close range, dynamite at the centre) mark 3 boxes: enough to take an unhurt character straight to Incapacitated and Dying.
                  • Explosions mark 1 box on everyone nearby and their full boxes at the centre; Dodge means diving clear.
                  • Jams. A gun jams on a natural 18; clearing it takes an action (see Guns and ammunition).

                  Armor, toughness and weaknesses

                  • Armor turns some hits. Protective gear makes a hit mark one fewer box, a set number of times, and its entry says what it works against: a Himmelsbrief turns one bullet a case.
                  • Toughness. A Wesen’s or a creature’s trait can say hits of 1 box mark nothing, hits of 2 or more boxes mark one fewer, or bullets and blades mark 1 box at most (a Siegbarste’s iron bones).
                  • Weaknesses. What a creature is weak to marks one box more, and its toughness and armor don’t help against it. Only fire marks more than 1 box means everything else marks at most 1. What an attack is made of (silver, cold iron, a Grimm’s old weapon, a poison) is what counts: say it when you attack. A weakness works whoever uses it, whether or not anyone knows of it.

                  Medical care

                  Frontier medicine in 1889 has chloroform, carbolic acid, laudanum and steady hands, and not much else. Wesen heal like humans, and some injuries need remedies from older traditions.

                  Diagnosis and stabilization

                  Roll Medicine when the diagnosis is uncertain or an urgent treatment decision matters. Emergency stabilization takes one action and a successful Medicine roll with a doctor’s bag. It ends Dying and stops deterioration, but removes no boxes.

                  Field treatment

                  With a doctor’s bag and several uninterrupted minutes, successful Medicine removes one injury box; success by 5 or more removes two.

                  Treatment attempts

                  Each set of injuries permits one field-treatment attempt and one proper-care attempt. Further rolls require a meaningful improvement in care, a new treatment, or recovery time. Simply waiting and trying again is insufficient.

                  • A set of injuries is the boxes from one fight or other harmful incident.
                  • Later injuries start a new set, with its own attempts, which remove only that set’s boxes. They don’t reopen wounds already treated.
                  • Another roll needs something new: a doctor’s surgery after field treatment, an Apothecary remedy for the poison under the wound, or days of rest before trying again.
                  • Stabilization is separate: never limited, and it never removes boxes.

                  A doctor’s surgery

                  A proper surgery with instruments, chloroform and a clean table grants +2 to Medicine. A successful treatment removes two boxes; success by 5 or more removes three. Allow the time the operation requires.

                  Remedies and poisons

                  Apothecary treats what Medicine can’t: Wesen poisons, curses, magical sickness and Wesen-specific ailments. Finding the right remedy usually means first identifying the cause (Wesen Lore or Occult), then brewing it (Apothecary, with an apothecary kit). A remedy can substitute for field treatment against such harm.

                  Natural recovery

                  Rest clears injury one box at a time, and the worse you’re hurt, the longer each box takes. Rest means no riding, no fighting, nothing strenuous.

                  Boxes marked Rest to clear one box
                  1 A full day
                  2 At least three days
                  3 A week, and only in someone’s care

                  At three boxes, rest alone does nothing until someone has stabilized or treated you; after that, a week of rest in their care clears the first box. A doctor’s treatment is faster. The times add up: from three boxes, rest alone takes about a week and a half to heal completely (a week, then three days, then a day).

                  Making your own

                  The callings and heritages in this book are a starting point, not a limit. With the GM’s agreement, any player can create their own calling or heritage. The creator has a Custom option at the end of each list, and its checklist holds a custom choice to the same rules as everything else.

                  A custom calling

                  A calling is a concept, not a package of powers: it gives no bonus of its own. A custom one only needs a clear place in the group.

                  1. Name it and describe it. One sentence for what your character does that no one else does, in the style of the callings above: “Undertaker: buries the dead, and makes sure they stay buried.”
                  2. Choose a group. Pick the one it belongs with: The Law, The Learned or The Frontier. The group only sets your sheet’s colors and emblem.
                  3. Pick four core skills. The skills it can’t do without. Mark them Trained yourself in step 3 (the built-in callings do this for you); they count toward your normal Trained skills.
                  4. Check it with the GM. It should fit the setting and overlap the existing callings as little as possible.

                  In the creator: in step 1, choose Custom calling… at the end of the Calling list, then enter its name and group. Write the description in What you bring.

                  A custom heritage

                  Build it with the GM, one step at a time.

                  1. The concept

                  Two or three sentences: where they come from, what sets them apart, and how others see them. It should be something the heritages in this book don’t already cover.

                  2. The tier

                  The tier measures how much the heritage gives on its own. Each tier costs one of your 3 Edge points, so stronger heritages start with fewer Edges.

                  Tier Edges Use it for
                  0 3 Ordinary people: a homeland, upbringing or culture, with no special abilities
                  1 2 One small, constant edge: keen senses, a tough hide, a free Trained skill, resistance to one hazard
                  2 1 Clear inhuman or supernatural gifts: venom, a second form, strong resistances
                  3 0 Remarkable beings: several strong abilities, and serious weaknesses to match

                  When in doubt, choose the higher tier.

                  3. Attribute adjustments

                  Give adjustments from −2 to +2 per attribute that total zero: a +2 needs a −2 or two −1s. The adjustments move the attribute’s base (+1 starts it at 11) and raise its starting maximum (13 plus the bonus). Most heritages change two to four attributes.

                  4. Core rules (tier 1 and up)

                  One to three short rules that always apply: what they can do (see in the dark, resist cold, a free Trained skill) and what it costs (a weakness, a people’s distrust, a hazard they can’t abide). A strength with no drawback is worth more than it looks, so price it a tier higher.

                  5. Heritage advantages

                  Write one to three. Each works like a general advantage: +2 to one skill in a narrow situation, or a small, limited permission (“once per scene…”). Each comes with a companion complication: a problem the trait causes, with a clear trigger and a real consequence, which earns a Grit point when it bites, like any complication. A character takes one advantage, general or heritage, so make them distinct choices.

                  6. A woge (optional, tier 2 or 3)

                  If the heritage changes shape, give the form attribute bonuses (usually +2 to one attribute and +1 to another), two or three traits, and its drawbacks. The shared rules for forms in The woge and the hidden world apply.

                  In the creator

                  1. In step 2, choose Custom heritage… at the end of the Heritage list.
                  2. Name it, choose the tier you agreed, and set its adjustments. The checklist confirms they total zero.
                  3. Write its core rules in Other ability (GM approval required) in step 4.
                  4. In step 4, choose Custom heritage advantage… and enter the advantage and its companion complication.

Companion: Wesen Kin: Wesen recognize their own, and some want you back in the fold; accept the visit at the worst time.

                  Quick reference

                  Creation: attributes start at 10, adjusted by heritage (always totaling zero), plus 5 points; maximum 13 plus any heritage bonus. 3 points split between heritage tier and Edges (Tier 0: 3 Edges; Tier 3: none). Well-rounded: one Specialty and nine Trained. Old hand: one Expert at +5 and seven Trained. One advantage (general or heritage) and one complication; an optional Allegiance.

                  Skills: Untrained = attribute −4; Trained = attribute +1; Specialty = attribute +3; Expert (Old hand) = attribute +5.

                  Modifiers: favorable +2; ordinary 0; difficult −2; very difficult −4; extreme −6. Natural 3–4 succeeds; natural 17–18 fails.

                  Assistance: success +1; success by 5 or more +2; 18 causes a complication. One assistant per roll.

                  Move: 5 yd, +1 per 2 points of Agility + Body above 20, +1 Trained Athletics or +2 Specialty/Expert, ± heritage speed; run (no action) twice that. Threats 8 yd. Wounded −2 yd; seriously wounded half, no run. Hard going: half. Stepping away is free. Reach: 1 yd fists, knives, claws; 2 yd axes, sabers; 3 yd spears. Horse 15 yd (gallop 40); −2 from a moving horse.

                  Range: Close 10 yd, Short 25, Medium 100, Long 300, Extreme 1,000. One band beyond a weapon’s −2, two −4, farther out of range.

                  Combat: move plus one action. Hit → one defense a round (Dodge, Parry or Block; −1 to −3 against a clean hit; Additional Defense: a second, at −2) → weapon effect. Dodge = Agility −2 plus Athletics training (+0, +1, +2). Telling hit (by 5+ or natural 3–4): the attacker picks an effect the weapon can deliver (pin: weapon in hand, claws or grapple only), which never adds boxes or kills outright; called shot −2 (small target −4).

                  Fine weapon or tool: +2 to one narrow, agreed use of a skill; never with an advantage on the same roll; never to Dodge, Parry or Block.

                  Damage: fists, clubs, knives and arrows 1 box; guns, heavy blades and Wesen claws 2; a close-range shotgun or buffalo rifle 3 and Dying. A 2-box weapon that brings you to three boxes leaves you Dying; at three boxes, another 2-box hit kills.

                  Guns: derringer 2, revolver 6, shotgun 2, rifle 15. Reload: one action. Natural 18 jams.

                  Medicine: stabilize in one action. Field care clears 1 box (2 on margin 5+). A surgery gives +2 and clears 2 (3 on margin 5+). Each set of injuries (one fight or incident) gets one field and one proper-care attempt; another needs better care, a new treatment or time. Rest clears a box in a day at 1 box, three days at 2, a week at 3 (in someone’s care).

                  Woge: free action for a Wesen; a forced woge on a failed Will roll. The woge profile’s bonuses apply while woged. Grimms see woge under strong emotion. A full woge before townsfolk marks Exposure (2 boxes at Tier 3).

                  Rituals: describe the effect; fill a clock of 2, 4 or 6 segments with Occult rolls at your Lens’s pace. Trappings remove a segment. Charms: Trained 2, Specialty 3, Expert 4.

                  Grit: start each session with two (three with True grit). Reroll, reduce an injury by one box, shrug off a telling hit, or establish a small preparation. Earn one from an accepted complication.

                  The first case: The Larder at Hoffnungstal

                  A starter case for three to seven, for one or two sessions. Children are vanishing from a Volga German colony, a mob blames the Blutbad family down the road, and the thing taking them is somebody else entirely. It shows off the diary and its clocks, the woge and the Code of Swabia, the Trouble tracks, and a Grimm’s hardest question: what the town is allowed to know. It’s written for the seven ready-made characters, and works with any.

                  The hook. After dark, Pastor Jakob Unruh knocks on Lena Baer’s door. Three children have gone missing from Hoffnungstal, the Volga German colony east of town, in three weeks. The colonists say a wolf took them, and they’ve found a wolf to blame: the Raebers, a German family homesteading by the creek, who keep to themselves. Tonight the men are talking about rope. Give each of them a reason of their own:

                  • Magdalena “Lena” Baer: Pastor Unruh brings his people’s hard cases to her, and this one is beyond him.
                  • Eliza Kessler: a lynching is a federal matter when the badge is in town, and a Wesen killing is a matter for a Grimm.
                  • Gus Brandt: Old Raeber ran with Gus’s pack, long ago. Gus knows the family is Blutbad, and that they gave up hunting when he did.
                  • Roz Vey: one of the missing children’s mothers buys tea at her shop every week, and hasn’t come in.
                  • Dr. Thaddeus Whitlock: the colony’s doctor sent for him to look at a dog torn open in a yard. The wounds bother him.
                  • Cora Ridgeway: she knows the creek country, and she’s seen tracks out there that walk like a man and sink like a bear.
                  • Silas Thorne: the miller who owns half the colony’s debts, Konrad Weiss, plays cards with Silas every Thursday, and won big last week. Konrad never wins.

                  What’s really happening. Konrad Weiss, Hoffnungstal’s miller, came west after the famine on the Volga, and in the worst of that winter he ate what he shouldn’t have. He came back from it a Wendigo. He hid it for years; this autumn the hunger won. He takes the children alive and keeps them in a cold cellar under the old mill race, his larder. Two are still alive. The Raebers know something is out there hunting: their son Emil has been out at night, in woge, trying to find it, and that is what the colony saw.

                  1. Hoffnungstal. A neat colony of whitewashed houses, a church, a mill on the creek. Mothers at the church door. Men in the street outside the Raeber land, with lanterns. Start a Lynch mob clock of 6 (Suspicion has done its work), and the threat clock for the case: the Wendigo will take another child when it fills. Eliza’s badge, Lena’s standing with the pastor, or Silas’s tongue can hold the mob for a night: a Persuasion or Leadership roll pushes the clock back one.

                  2. The Raebers. Old Raeber meets them at the door with a shotgun, and Gus with a nod. Emil has been out every night, hunting the thing. He’s seen it: tall, thin, wrong, and it calls out in a child’s voice. The Raebers won’t woge in front of strangers, but they’ll trust a Grimm who keeps the Code.

                  3. The diary. Start a discovery clock for the thing taking the children (The diaries, in the Players’ Guide). The clues, and who finds them best:

                  • The dog (Medicine, Wesen Lore): torn open, and the heart is gone. A Blutbad kills for the hunt; this was hunger.
                  • The tracks by the creek (Tracking, Survival): two legs, too long a stride, and frost in the prints though the night was mild.
                  • The colony’s stories (Read People, Persuasion with the pastor’s help): the old women talk of the famine winter, and of a man who came back from it changed. They won’t say who.
                  • Konrad’s mill (Investigation, or Silas at the card table): the flour sacks are stacked to hide a door. Konrad’s hands shake when he looks at children; his winnings went on salt and lye.
                  • Roz’s shop (Apothecary): Konrad has bought a remedy for “a hunger that won’t stop” three times this year.

                  When the Identify phase is done, they know what it is: a Wendigo, and that it takes its victims alive. Filling the clock earns the diary entry on how it’s stopped: fire, and a body burned.

                  4. The night hunt. If the threat clock fills, another child is gone, and the mob clock jumps two. Emil Raeber is seen in woge near the empty bed, and the colony comes for him with rope. The characters can be there first, or not.

                  5. The larder. A Survival roll follows the frost-tracks to the old mill race, and the cold cellar under it. Two children alive, half-frozen; Dr. Whitlock and Roz can keep them that way.

                  6. Konrad. He comes home to find them in his larder. In woge, he’s a Wendigo: Claws (2), Stealth 14, and the voices of the children he took. This is the big fight, in a dark mill with flour in the air and fire close to hand. Lena’s Himmelsbrief and her charms are worth more here than a gun.

                  7. The Code. The mob arrives at the mill as the fight ends, with lanterns. What do they see? If they see the woge, the Raebers are cleared, but Exposure marks a box and the Wesen Council will hear of it. If the characters hide it, Konrad becomes a man who died in a fire, and the town still suspects the Raebers unless someone gives it a better story (Deception), and Suspicion stays. Eliza’s badge says the law should decide; the Code says the town must never know.

                  Rewards. Two children home. A diary entry on the Wendigo. The Raebers’ trust, and Gus’s old pack hears he did right by them. Pastor Unruh owes Lena a great deal, and his colony owes the Raebers an apology they may never make.

                  Threats. Wendigo (Konrad Weiss); the mob as Hired guns (minor) who won’t shoot a lawman first; Blutbad in full woge (Emil Raeber, if it comes to a misunderstanding).

                  Your own threats, weapons and Edges

                  A GM can add their own. Keep them in line with the book’s:

                  • Threats. A minor foe has 1 box; an ordinary one 3; a tough one 4 or 5; a rare horror 6. Attack targets run 11 to 13 for most, 14 or 15 for the dangerous. Dodge runs 7 to 10. Give it one special trait, said plainly, and decide what it does before play: toughness, a weakness or armor works as in Armor, toughness and weaknesses.
                  • Weapons. Hits mark 1 box for fists, knives and arrows; 2 for sabers and guns; 3, and Dying, for the heavy and the point-blank. Give a weapon a reach (melee) or a range band, and its rounds if they run out.
                  • Edges. Worth about as much as one of the book’s: a narrow, useful benefit, often once per session or per case. Say exactly what it does.
                  • Spells and rituals. A new spell matches the book’s (an attack marks 1 or 2 boxes at Close or Short range; anything stronger says who resists); a ritual has its weight (Minor 2 segments, Significant 4, Major 6), the time a roll takes, what it does and its price.

                  In the creator. The GM view’s Homebrew tab keeps your threats, weapons, Edges, heritages, advantages, spells and rituals. They then work like the book’s, marked homebrew. Save them to a file to keep them or share them with another GM.

                  The GM’s tools

                  The battle map

                  The GM view of the character creator has a battle map for fights where it helps to see who is where. It is a tool, not a rule: everything on it follows the Personal combat rules in the Players’ Guide, and a fight without it works the same way. Open How to use the map on the map itself for the buttons, step by step.

                  Two kinds of map.

                  • A hex map for fights where position matters: cover, doorways, who can reach whom. The GM chooses what one hex is: 1 yard for a brawl in a saloon, 5 or 10 yards for a street, 50 for open prairie. Dragging a token or measuring shows the distance and its range band, so the GM can read the penalty from the Range table in the Players’ Guide. Up close it names the reach: a knife or claws at 1 yard, a saber at 2, a spear at 3. When both the attacker and the target have tokens on the map (a character by their card, anyone else by the same name as in the Encounter panel), the panel checks the distance against the weapon: in reach, close enough to move in and strike, too far this turn, or for a gun the range band, which it sets. A token can have a height (a rider on a bluff, a shooter in a church tower), and the distance counts it.
                  • A range track for fights where only distance matters: a chase after a stagecoach, a running fight across open country. It has a lane for the posse and one for each range band. Moving a token into the next lane moves it one band in or out.

                  Tokens. The GM places the posse with one button. Their tokens stay linked to the characters in the GM view: marking Injury, Dying, Dazed or Grit points on a token marks it on the character, and the other way round. Opponents come from the Threats table with their boxes filled in. Their pips fill as they are hurt, and a token whose boxes are all marked is taken out.

                  Keeping a map. The map stays in the GM’s browser. Save map to file keeps both maps, their pictures and tokens in one file, to prepare a fight ahead of time or share it with another GM.

                  The Discovery panel

                  The GM view’s Discovery: the diaries panel runs discovery clocks. Start a clock with its segments per phase (or a plain progress clock for a task in the same race), mark segments as the characters succeed, name the subject at the gate, and choose the primary investigator. When the clock fills, write what they established and mint it: the primary investigator owns the diary entry and everyone present gets a shared copy. Record a finding without a clock does the same for a direct finding, and marks the cost you choose: a box of Trouble, or a tick on a danger clock or the threat clock. The threat clock stays idle until you start it. Save the characters’ files afterwards so the players get their entries. A step of the campaign builder’s outline can wait for diary entries, as its trigger.

                  The Encounter panel

                  The GM view also has an Encounter panel that runs a fight by the Personal combat rules and shows every roll. Add characters from the GM view and opponents from the Threats table (or make one up on the spot), then start: it keeps the turn order by Agility, and for each attack works out the range, the defense (Dodge, Parry or Block, one a round, and a second at −2 with Additional Defense), the boxes marked and any telling hit (offering only the effects the weapon can deliver), offering a Grit point choices at the moment they apply. Hits land on the character cards and the battle map, and with gritty combat on, grievous wounds are rolled and kept. The panel keeps a log and a summary of the fight, which can be saved to a file. It’s a helper, not a rule: the GM can rule any result differently, and the log says so. For each attack, choose what it’s made of (silver, a wooden stake or fire), and the panel applies the rules that turn on it: a Wesen’s Tough in full woge (and what gets past it), and a creature only the right weapon can hurt, like a Siegbarste’s iron bones.

                  The shared table

                  The GM can start a shared table from the GM view and give the players its code; each player joins from their character’s Play tab. While they’re connected, every roll from a sheet is rolled by the table server, so the whole posse sees the same dice as they land, in a live log on the Play tab and on the GM view. A Grit reroll answers its own roll, once.

                  Each player’s character is shared with the GM too: it appears among the GM view’s characters, marked at the table, and its injuries, Grit, rounds, full woge and lasting wounds stay in step both ways, whether they’re changed on the sheet, on the GM’s card or in an encounter. In a fight, each player’s Play tab shows the GM’s battle map and the encounter’s turn order and latest results; a player drags their own token, and on their turn sends the GM what they’ll do, which the GM puts into the Encounter panel and runs.

                  The GM can remove a player from the table, and give the table a new code: everyone at it moves with it, and the old code stops working. Without a table, the roller works exactly as it always has.

                      Quick reference

                      Resolution
                      3d6 at or below adjusted skill. Natural 3–4 always succeeds; 17–18 always fails. Margin = target − roll.
                      Opposed
                      Success beats failure; both succeed → larger margin wins; ties and double failures favor the defender.
                      Combat
                      Move a short distance and take one action. Hit → target may defend once per round (Dodge, Parry or Block; two with Additional Defense) → weapon effect. Partial cover −2.
                      Defending
                      One defense a round (two with Additional Defense). Parry: Melee −3 with a weapon (fists, claws, fangs, blades); Brawling −3 bare-handed against fists only; a second parry −2 more. Block: Melee −2 with a chair or slat, −1 with a shield, against blades, claws, fists and arrows (never bullets); an improvised shield breaks on a 2+ box hit. Attack succeeded by 3–4: defense −1; 5–6: −2; 7+: −3. Aim (one action): +1 to your next attack on that target.
                      Telling hit
                      Hit by 5+ or a natural 3–4, not defended: the attacker picks one effect (disarm, knock down, pin, rattle −2, break gear, open them up +2, precise). Called shot: −2 (small target −4), and any hit gets the effect. No extra boxes. Shrug it off: Hard to put down (once a fight) or a Grit point.
                      Damage
                      Fists, clubs, knives, arrows: 1 box. Guns, heavy blades, Wesen claws: 2. Close-range shotgun or buffalo rifle: 3 and Dying.
                      Dying
                      A 2-box weapon that brings you to 3 boxes: Dying; dead at the end of the scene unless stabilized (one action, Medicine, doctor's bag). At 3 boxes, another 2-box hit kills.
                      Guns
                      Derringer 2, revolver 6, shotgun 2, rifle 15. Fan the hammer 3 (−2, +1 box), both barrels 2 (+2 close), covering fire 3. Reload: one action. Natural 18 jams.
                      Woge
                      Free action for Wesen; strong emotion may force one (Will roll). Grimms see it. A full woge before townsfolk marks Exposure.
                      Medicine
                      Stabilize: one action. Field care clears 1 box (2 on margin 5+). A surgery gives +2, clears 2 (3 on margin 5+).
                      Rest
                      Clearing one box: a full day at 1 box, at least three days at 2, a week at 3 (and only once someone has stabilized or treated you).
                      Grit
                      Reroll (keep second), reduce an injury by one box, shrug off a telling hit, or establish a small preparation.
