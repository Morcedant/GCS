You are a member of Starfleet aboard USS Pelion, NCC-1750. Explore unfamiliar worlds, investigate strange phenomena, protect your crewmates, and make difficult decisions when orders and circumstances collide. Your training matters, and the crew succeeds by working together.

This guide presents the Pelion rules in a form you can use at the table. It combines concrete skills and 3d6 roll-under resolution with a small pool of Duty points.

## The rule to remember

Roll 3d6 at or below your adjusted skill to succeed. Your skill already includes your training and attribute. Do not add the attribute again.

*Example:* Sensors 13, with a −2 penalty for interference, gives a target of 11. A roll of 9 succeeds by 2. A roll of 12 fails by 1.

## What this edition adds

This revision keeps every rule from the original guide and adds these campaign rules:

- **General Services**, a division for enlisted crew holding Crewman and Yeoman ranks.
- **Species attribute adjustments**, which shift starting attributes and always total zero.
- **Species advantages**, which can replace your regular advantage and always come with a companion complication.
- **Custom species**, built with the GM under the same limits.
- **Defenses, telling hits and called shots:** Parry and Block beside Dodge, and great hits that decide what happens.
- **Advancement points**, earned after missions and spent on skills, advantages and attributes.
- **Gritty combat**, an optional rule for grievous wounds.
- **A battle map** in the GM view, for away teams and ship fights.

## Guide scope

Personal actions and away-team encounters are covered here. Starship stations use the same skills. Species abilities beyond the adjustments and species advantages in this guide still require GM approval. Ship combat uses the light rules and the ship registry in *The USS Pelion*.

# Create your character

Work through the five steps in order. The USS Pelion Officer Creator follows the same steps and checks the rules for you.

## Step 1: Choose your role

Choose a department and describe what you contribute to the crew. Rank and assignment are agreed with the GM; rank gives no mechanical bonus.

| Department | Division | Uniform | Typical contribution | Core skills |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Command | Command | Gold | Coordinate the crew and negotiate | Leadership, Diplomacy, Psychology, Phasers |
| Helm | Command | Gold | Fly the ship and her shuttles | Helm, Navigation, Sensors, Phasers |
| Navigation | Command | Gold | Plot courses and fix position | Navigation, Sensors, Helm, Computers |
| Science | Sciences | Blue | Read and explain anomalies | Sensors, Physical Sciences, Life Sciences, Computers |
| Medical | Sciences | Blue | Diagnose, stabilize, and treat | Medicine, Life Sciences, Psychology, Sensors |
| Engineering | Operations | Red | Keep machinery and power running | Engineering, Electronics, Computers, Physical Sciences |
| Security | Operations | Red | Protect the away team and the ship | Phasers, Unarmed Combat, Athletics, Stealth |
| Communications | Operations | Red | Establish contact and cut through interference | Communications, Electronics, Computers, Diplomacy |
| General Services | General Services | Red | Keep records, carry orders, handle the ship's daily business | Computers, Electronics, Athletics, Communications |

The creator marks your department's four **core skills** (in the table above) Trained when you choose it. Keep them, raise one to your Specialty, or swap any of them for other skills; changing department swaps the core skills you haven't changed.

### Officers and enlisted crew

Officers hold ranks from Ensign through Captain. **General Services** is the division for enlisted crew: Crewman 3rd, 2nd, and 1st Class, Crewman, and Yeoman 3rd, 2nd, and 1st Class, and Yeoman. A character with a Crewman or Yeoman rank belongs to General Services. Enlisted crew use the same creation rules as officers, except for their skills (see Step 3): they are specialists rather than generalists.

## Step 2: Choose a species and assign attributes

### Attributes

| Attribute | Used for |
|---|---|
| Body | Strength, endurance, resisting injury |
| Agility | Coordination, reflexes, movement |
| Intellect | Reasoning, observation, technical understanding |
| Presence | Leadership, persuasion, social influence |
| Will | Resolve, concentration, resisting fear and mental intrusion |

Every attribute starts at 10. Your species then adjusts some of them; the result is your **species base**. Each species' adjustments total zero, so no species starts ahead of another.

| Species | Adjustments |

| Vulcan | Body +1, Will +1, Presence −2 |
| Andorian | Agility +1, Will −1 |
| Tellarite | Body +1, Will +1, Agility −1, Presence −1 |
| Aenar | Will +1, Intellect +1, Body −1, Agility −1 |
| Illyrian | Body +1, Presence −1 |
| Lanthanite | Intellect +1, Agility −1 |
| Caitian | Agility +1, Body −1 |
| Aurelian | Agility +1, Intellect +1, Body −2 |
| Orion | Body +1, Presence +1, Intellect −1, Will −1 |
| Denobulan | Intellect +1, Presence +1, Body −1, Agility −1 |
| Trill | Intellect +1, Body −1 |
| Saurian | Body +1, Intellect +1, Agility −1, Presence −1 |

### Spending points

After applying your species, distribute **five additional points** among the attributes.

- The starting maximum is **13**, plus any species bonus to that attribute. A Vulcan can start with Body or Will at 14.
- You cannot lower an attribute below its species base to gain points.

### Species descriptions

**Human.** Adaptable and widespread across Starfleet. No adjustments; spend your points wherever you like.

**Vulcan.** Physically stronger than humans and trained from childhood in logic and emotional control. Their reserve can read as cold to other species.

**Andorian.** Blue-skinned, antennaed people from the icy moon Andoria. Quick and fiercely loyal, with a passionate, sometimes volatile temper.

**Tellarite.** Stocky, stubborn, and fond of argument as a form of respect. Hard to move once they have made up their minds.

**Aenar.** A blind, pale subspecies of Andorian with strong telepathic gifts and a pacifist culture. Hemmer served as the Enterprise's chief engineer.

**Illyrian.** A people who reshape themselves through genetic modification. Federation law bars augmented officers, so Illyrians in Starfleet, like Una Chin-Riley, have faced suspicion and secrecy.

**Lanthanite.** Extremely long-lived people who look human and have quietly lived on Earth for millennia, like Pelia. Centuries of experience; a body that dislikes hurrying.

**Caitian.** A feline species with keen senses and quick reflexes. Lt. M'Ress served aboard the Enterprise in this era.

**Aurelian.** A winged, avian people known as scholars and historians, like Aleek-Om. Capable of flight, but light-boned and physically frail.

**Orion.** Green-skinned people whose homeworld is better known for the Orion Syndicate's smugglers and pirates than for Starfleet service. Orions in uniform are rare in this era and often have to prove themselves.

**Denobulan.** Cheerful, sociable people with large extended families. They need little sleep most of the year, then hibernate for several days. Dr. Phlox served aboard Enterprise NX-01 a century earlier.

**Trill.** A spotted humanoid people, some of whom are joined with a long-lived symbiont that carries the memories of its earlier hosts. In this era the Trill keep joining a closely guarded secret from outsiders, Starfleet included. An unjoined Trill uses the same adjustments; the species advantages below need a symbiont.

**Saurian.** A reptilian people with tough scaled skin and large, dark eyes, known across the quadrant for Saurian brandy. Lt. Linus served aboard USS Discovery in the 2250s.

### Custom species

You can build your own species with the GM. See *Making your own* for the steps.

## Step 3: Choose trained skills

Choose ten different skills: one **Specialty** at its governing attribute +3 and nine **Trained** skills at attribute +1. All other skills are **Untrained** at attribute −4 when an attempt is plausible. Use these same three training levels for every skill. Each skill has its own final target number; do not add the attribute again when rolling.

### General Services: specialists

Enlisted crew in General Services trade breadth for depth. Instead of a Specialty and nine Trained skills, they choose **one Expert skill at its attribute +5** and **seven Trained skills at attribute +1**. Everything else is Untrained at attribute −4, as usual.

The extra bonus matters most when the work is hard. With Intellect 13, an Expert transporter chief has a target of 18, which only natural 17–18 can fail; under a −4 "very difficult" penalty the target is still 14, a 91% chance, where an officer's Specialty would be at 12, a 74% chance. If Athletics is the Expert skill, Dodge adds +2, the same as a Specialty.

**Earning a commission.** If a General Services character is commissioned (the GM's call, usually after distinguished service), they move to the officer package: the Expert skill becomes a Specialty at +3, and they can raise two more skills to Trained to reach nine. Rank and department change to match.

## Step 4: Describe your character

Choose your name, rank, department, and a short reason you serve in Starfleet. Then choose **one advantage** and **one complication**.

### Top 10 reasons for joining Starfleet

Pick one as a starting point and make it your own, or write something else entirely.

1. **Explore the unknown.** To see what's out there: strange new worlds and phenomena no one has charted yet.
2. **Family tradition.** Starfleet runs in my family, and I mean to live up to the name.
3. **Science and discovery.** The best laboratory in the galaxy is a starship on the frontier.
4. **Protect others.** To protect the Federation and the people who can't protect themselves.
5. **Inspired by a hero.** A Starfleet officer once saved my home, and I want to be that person for someone else.
6. **Believe in the ideals.** I believe in what the Federation stands for and want to serve it.
7. **Prove myself.** To prove I'm good enough, to everyone who doubted me and to myself.
8. **Leave the past behind.** To leave behind a past, a world, or a life that was too small for me.
9. **Make amends.** To make up for a mistake I can't undo.
10. **Build bridges.** To build bridges between my people and the rest of the Federation.

- **Advantage.** Choose either a general advantage or one of your species' advantages. A species advantage replaces the general advantage and always brings its companion complication.
- **Complication.** Choose one complication. You keep it even if you take a species advantage, so a character with a species advantage has two complications.

An advantage gives +2 to one narrowly defined kind of task when the GM agrees it applies. It cannot improve every use of a broad skill. A complication creates choices and problems; it is not a permanent penalty.

*Examples:* Steady Hands gives +2 to delicate medical procedures. Subspace Specialist gives +2 when analyzing subspace phenomena. Complications might be Cannot Leave a Mystery Alone, Distrusts Command Decisions, or Protects Others at Personal Risk. Define their triggers with the GM.

## Step 5: Prepare for duty

Your Dodge is Agility −2, plus your Athletics training bonus: +0 if Untrained, +1 if Trained, or +2 if a Specialty. Your Parry is Unarmed Combat −3, and your Block Unarmed Combat −2. Begin uninjured, with two Duty points and your assigned equipment. Describe one connection to another crew member.

### Top 10 connections to a crewmate

Pick one, name the crewmate with their player, and agree on the details together.

1. **Academy roommates.** We shared quarters at Starfleet Academy and still look out for each other.
2. **They saved my life.** They pulled me out of a burning shuttle, and I owe them my life.
3. **I saved theirs.** I saved their life on a past posting, and they haven't forgotten it.
4. **Mentor.** They took me under their wing when I first came aboard.
5. **Friendly rivals.** We competed for the same posting, and the rivalry is still mostly friendly.
6. **Same homeworld.** We grew up on the same world and know each other's families.
7. **An unpaid favor.** I owe them a favor I haven't repaid yet.
8. **A shared secret.** We share a secret from a mission neither of us talks about.
9. **Off-duty friends.** We meet every week in the rec room for chess, cards, or music.
10. **Unfinished business.** They blame me for something that went wrong on a past mission.

## Untrained actions

A plausible untrained attempt uses attribute −4. Specialized procedures such as surgery or warp-core repair require relevant training or direct guidance. Guidance permits an attempt only when the GM finds it credible; it does not automatically grant the expert's skill.

## Standard equipment

Equipment makes tasks possible rather than easier. Ordinary equipment grants no blanket bonus; exceptional tools or preparation may justify +2. Missing essential tools can make a task impossible, or very difficult (−4) when the GM finds an attempt plausible. Every crew member carries a communicator; the rest is issued for the mission.

| Item | What it does |
|---|---|
| Communicator | Talks to the ship and the away team within range, and gives the transporter a lock. Interference, shielding or distance can cut it off. |
| Tricorder | Scans life signs, energy, materials and atmosphere at short range and records the data. Field Sensors rolls need one; without it most scans are impossible. |
| Medical scanner | Identifies ordinary injuries automatically. Diagnosing unfamiliar physiology or disease without one is very difficult (−4) or impossible. |
| Medical kit | Hyposprays, drugs and dressings. Needed for emergency stabilization and field treatment. |
| Engineering kit | Hand tools, spare parts and test gear. Needed for most Engineering and Electronics repairs in the field. |
| Hand phaser | Type 1 (8 power) or Type 2 (16). Settings and power costs are under Phaser power. |
| Universal translator | Lets you talk with speakers of known languages. A new language takes time to learn; a Communications roll can speed it up. |
| Environmental suit | Several hours in vacuum, toxic air, cold or radiation. −2 to Agility-based actions and Stealth while worn. |
| Field kit | Rations, water, light, rope and climbing gear for a few days. Turns impossible survival and climbing tasks into ordinary ones; no bonus beyond that. |
| Emergency transponder | Broadcasts your position to the ship and helps a transporter lock through light interference. |
| Anti-grav sled | Carries heavy loads or a casualty at walking pace. Hard to move quietly (−2 Stealth). |

A tricorder finds readings; interpreting unusual results may still need a science skill. Spare power cells for phasers are listed under Phaser power.

Transporters, computers, and scanners have limits set by the situation. State the information or destination you need. A successful scan reveals useful evidence, not necessarily the complete explanation.

# Actions and teamwork

## When to roll

Routine work succeeds automatically when you have appropriate training, equipment, and time. Roll when failure could change the situation. Before rolling, establish your goal, the relevant skill, any modifiers, and what is at stake.

| Circumstances | Modifier |
|---|---|
| Favorable conditions or excellent preparation | +2 |
| Ordinary challenge under pressure | 0 |
| Difficult | −2 |
| Very difficult | −4 |
| Extreme | −6 |

Apply the main circumstance modifier once. Add separately specified effects such as assistance or injury when they apply. The GM avoids charging twice for the same obstacle.

## Results and margins

Add the three dice. A total at or below the adjusted skill succeeds. Margin of success equals the adjusted skill minus the roll. A larger margin may mean better information, more precise work, or a stronger position; agree any special threshold before rolling.

A natural 3 or 4 always succeeds. A natural 17 or 18 always fails. These are automatic outcomes, not a separate critical-hit system. An impossible action receives no roll.

In an opposed contest, each side rolls its relevant skill. One success beats one failure. If both succeed, the larger success margin wins. A tie, or two failures, favors the defender or preserves the situation. For an automatic success above its target, count its contest margin as zero.

## Assistance

One qualified assistant can roll a relevant skill before the lead character rolls. Success grants +1 to the lead roll; success by 5 or more grants +2. Failure gives no bonus. An 18 introduces a complication described by the GM. Additional crew members can perform other tasks but cannot stack assistance bonuses on the same roll.

*Example:* Engineering detects that an alien device is building an overload. A science officer assists with Physical Sciences 14 and rolls 9, succeeding by 5. The engineer receives +2 to the repair attempt. Each officer contributes a different kind of expertise.

## Failure and another attempt

Failure changes the situation: time passes, information remains incomplete, resources are consumed, or danger increases. Do not repeat an unchanged roll until it succeeds. A new approach, new evidence, better tools, or additional time may justify another attempt.

## Success at a cost

When a roll fails by only **1 or 2**, the GM may offer **success at a cost**: you get what you were after, but something else goes wrong. Typical costs are a Wounded box, a damaged tool or ship system, lost time (tick a clock), a new problem in the scene, or someone else noticing. The GM names the cost before you accept; you can always take the plain failure instead.

A natural 17 or 18 is never a success at a cost. A cost that comes from your complication can still earn a Duty point, as usual.

*Example:* Lieutenant Okafor needs 12 or less to lock the transporter on a falling shuttle and rolls 13. The GM offers success at a cost: the crew beams out, but the transporter burns out a relay and is offline until Engineering repairs it. Okafor accepts.

## Progress clocks

A **clock** is a circle cut into 4, 6 or 8 segments that tracks something that takes time or builds toward disaster: *Warp core breach (4)*, *Repair the shuttle (6)*, *Klingon reinforcements arrive (4)*, *Win the council's trust (8)*.

- **Danger clocks** fill when things go wrong: a failed roll, a success at a cost, or time passing. When one fills, the danger happens.
- **Progress clocks** fill as the crew succeeds: one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. When one fills, the goal is achieved.
- The GM sizes the clock (4 = urgent or simple, 6 = substantial, 8 = long or complex) and shows it to the players, so everyone can see the pressure building.

Clocks are how a failed roll changes the situation without stopping the story. Racing a progress clock against a danger clock makes a strong climax.

## Investigation and social scenes

Ask concrete questions and explain your approach. Evidence may require several different tasks. Diplomacy can secure cooperation or concessions, while Psychology offers insight into behavior. A successful roll does not force someone to abandon a core belief or provide information they do not possess.

# Personal combat

## Turn order and actions

Act in descending Agility order. Players choose the order of tied crew members; the GM resolves other ties. A round lasts a few seconds. On your turn, move a short distance and take one meaningful action, such as attacking, using equipment, helping, or attempting a task. The GM judges movement by the scene; there is no fixed grid scale.

## Attacking and defending

Roll Phasers for a ranged attack, or Unarmed Combat for fists, grappling and a weapon in hand. If the attack succeeds, an aware target able to move may **defend**: Dodge, Parry or Block. A successful defense cancels the hit. Apply injury penalties to every defense.

- **One defense a round.** A target gets one defense a round, between its turns, and chooses which when an attack hits. The **Additional Defense** advantage gives a second.
- **Dodge** is Agility −2, with +1 for Trained Athletics or +2 for Athletics as a Specialty or Expert. Untrained Athletics adds nothing; use the training level, not the final Athletics score.
- **Parry.** Against a weapon in hand or fists, roll Unarmed Combat −3 with a weapon in hand: a d'k tahg, a lirpa, a length of pipe. Bare-handed, Unarmed Combat −3 parries fists only. A second parry in the same round (with Additional Defense) is a further −2. Nobody parries a phaser beam or a disruptor bolt.
- **Block.** With anything that can stand in for a shield (a chair, a crate lid, a torn-off panel), you can block a weapon in hand, fists or a thrown weapon: roll Unarmed Combat −2. Nothing you can lift blocks an energy weapon. An improvised shield breaks the first time it blocks a hit of 2 or more boxes.
- **Attack quality.** A clean hit is harder to escape. When an attack succeeds by 3 or 4, the defense is at −1; by 5 or 6, −2; by 7 or more, −3.
- **Aim.** Spend your action aiming at a target you can see: +1 to your next attack against it. **Careful Marksman** adds its +2 to this.

Solid cover blocks a shot. Partial cover imposes −2 on the attack. Other difficult conditions use the normal modifier scale. Weapon effects apply after a hit survives any defense. Success margin does not increase damage: a great hit decides *what* happens instead.

## Telling hits

A really good shot, or a blow that lands exactly where it was meant to, lets the attacker say what it does.

- **A telling hit** is an attack that succeeds by **5 or more**, or on a natural 3 or 4, and isn't stopped by a defense. The attacker picks **one effect** below and describes it. The weapon still marks its usual boxes.
- **A called shot.** Before rolling, name the effect you want and take −2 on the attack, or −4 for a small target (a hand, a phaser, a control panel, a power cell). If the attack hits at all, the effect happens, whatever the margin. **Careful Marksman** cuts this penalty by 1.
- **Phasers on stun** land telling hits too. The effect happens whether or not the target resists the stun.
- **Both ways.** Klingons, pirates and stranger things land telling hits on the crew too.

| Effect | What happens |
|---|---|
| Disarm | The weapon or held item drops, or flies a few yards away |
| Knock down | The target falls prone, or is driven back a few steps |
| Pin | The target is held, tangled or pinned down: their next action goes to getting free |
| Rattle | −2 to the target's next roll |
| Break gear | A phaser, tricorder, communicator or improvised shield breaks |
| Open them up | The next attack on this target this round gets +2 |
| Precise | You hit exactly what you aimed at: the control panel, the power coupling, the hand on the trigger |

**Limits.** A telling hit never adds boxes and never gets past a defense. The effect has to make sense: nobody disarms a Gorn of its claws, and the GM can rule an effect out. Opponents who drop from one hit are already down, so a telling hit against them sets up what comes next.

**Staying alive.** A crew member on the receiving end can shrug off a telling hit's effect with **Hard to put down** (once per fight) or by spending a Duty point. Either way, they still take the boxes.

*Example:* a Klingon warrior slashes at Lt. Reyes with a d'k tahg and succeeds by 5. Reyes hasn't defended yet this round: Dodge 12, −2 for the clean hit, needs a 10 or less. Bare-handed, Reyes can't parry a blade, so Reyes dodges. When the second Klingon swings before Reyes's next turn, there's no defense left, unless Reyes has Additional Defense.

## Range

Distances fall into five bands:

| Band | Distance | For example |
|---|---|---|
| Close | Up to 10 yards | Across a room or a corridor junction; one move covers it |
| Short | Up to 50 yards | Across a shuttle bay or a village square |
| Medium | Up to 150 yards | Across a cargo hold or a city block |
| Long | Up to 500 yards | Across a canyon or a landing field |
| Extreme | Up to a mile, in line of sight | Ridge to ridge |

Every ranged weapon has an **effective band**, shown in the weapons table. Attacks within it take no penalty. One band beyond it is −2, two bands beyond is −4, and anything farther is out of range. Melee weapons and fists reach only someone next to you. Your move each turn covers the Close band; give up your action to run and you cover Short.

## Injury

| Boxes marked | Condition | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Uninjured | No injury penalty |
| 1 | Wounded | −1 to physical actions |
| 2 | Seriously wounded | −2 to physical actions |
| 3 | Incapacitated | Unable to act; medical aid needed |

A normal damaging hit marks one box. A powerful attack can mark two, and a killing weapon marks three (see Weapons and damage). Injury penalties replace each other rather than stacking. They apply to movement, attacks, Dodge, and other tasks the injury physically hinders. The GM announces especially lethal hazards before resolution where possible.

### Dying and death

Two boxes hurt; they don't kill. Death comes from three things:

- **Dying.** A hit from a killing weapon (a phaser or disruptor on kill) that takes you to three boxes leaves you Incapacitated and **Dying**. A Dying character dies at the end of the scene unless someone stabilizes them: one action and a successful Medicine roll with a medical kit. Once stabilized, you are still Incapacitated but no longer Dying.
- **A further lethal hit.** While you are at three boxes, any hit from a weapon that marks two or more boxes (a heavy blade, a phaser or disruptor on kill, an explosion's centre) kills you. Fists, clubs and knives just keep you down.
- **Disintegration** and comparable hazards bypass the track entirely.

Your protection is avoiding the hit: Dodge, cover, and a Duty point, which reduces a kill shot from three boxes to two, leaving you Seriously wounded but on your feet.

### Optional rule: gritty combat

For a harder, bloodier game, the GM can turn on **gritty combat** for the campaign (the creator has a switch on the Condition card and in the GM view). The rest of the rules stay the same.

**Grievous hits.** A hit that marks **2 or more boxes** is **grievous** when the attack succeeded by **7 or more**, rolled a **natural 3 or 4**, or left the target **Dying**.

- **A crew member** rolls 3d6 on the table below and suffers the result on top of the boxes.
- **An opponent** is simply taken out: no roll, no boxes to count. Describe how, or let the GM view roll a description.
- **With a telling hit,** the attacker also chooses which limb.

| 3d6 | Grievous wound | Effect | Heals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3–4 | Lost limb | A hand, an arm or a leg is gone; the GM says which. −4 to tasks that need it, or impossible. Dying unless someone stops the bleeding. | Never: a complication (a prosthetic, at a starbase) |
| 5–6 | Lost eye | −2 to Phasers and to spotting things. | Never: a complication |
| 7–9 | Broken bone | The limb is useless until a bone-knitter sets it in sickbay. | A day in sickbay; several weeks without |
| 10–12 | Torn muscle | −1 to physical tasks. | A few days with sickbay care; several weeks without |
| 13–15 | Concussion | Stunned, and −2 to thinking tasks for the scene. | After the scene |
| 16–18 | Internal injury or severe burns | Dying even with boxes to spare; it needs sickbay. | A week after sickbay |

- **Duty points.** Spend a Duty point to **downgrade** a grievous wound one step: a lost limb becomes a broken bone, a lost eye a concussion, a broken bone or an internal injury a torn muscle, and a torn muscle or a concussion nothing lasting.
- **A limb for a life.** Once a fight, a crew member who would be left Dying or killed can **lose a limb, or the use of one**, instead. They live, Incapacitated but stable, and the limb becomes a complication. It replaces any grievous wound from the same hit.
- **Healing.** Grievous wounds heal on their own clock, whatever the boxes say. A lost limb or a lost eye never heals on its own.

## Weapons and damage

How many boxes a hit marks depends on the **weapon**, not on how well the attack roll succeeded. Apply it only after the hit survives any defense. This list sorts common weapons into one-box, two-box and killing (three-box) categories; the GM places anything else by comparison.

| Weapon | Range | Boxes on a hit | What the injury looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Punch, kick, headbutt | Melee | 1 | Bruising, a split lip, cracked ribs |
| Club, rifle butt, thrown rock | Melee; rock Close thrown | 1 | A concussion, a broken finger, heavy bruising |
| Knife or dagger, such as a Klingon d'k tahg | Melee; Close thrown | 1 | A cut or stab wound that bleeds |
| Arrow, spear, or primitive firearm | Medium; spear Close thrown | 1 | A puncture or gunshot wound |
| Heavy melee weapon, such as a Vulcan lirpa, a sword, or an axe | Melee | 2 | A deep wound or a broken bone |
| Phaser on stun | Short (type 1), Medium (type 2) | 0 | No wound; the target makes the stun roll (see Phaser settings) |
| Phaser on kill | Short (type 1), Medium (type 2) | 3, and Dying | Severe burns and energy trauma |
| Klingon disruptor | Medium | 3, and Dying | Severe burns and tissue disruption |
| Phaser rifle, or a ship's weapon fired at a person | Long; ship's weapon Extreme | 3 and Dying, or disintegration if the GM rules it | Massive energy trauma |
| Explosion | Close (thrown grenade) | 1 to everyone nearby, 2 at the centre | Blast, shrapnel, and burns; Dodge means diving clear, so you need somewhere to go |
| Fall | — | 1 for a bad fall, 2 for a long one | A sprain or a fracture |

*Example:* a Klingon's punch leaves Ensign Reyes Wounded (−1). On the next round a d'k tahg slash marks a second box: Seriously wounded (−2). A disruptor shot instead, on an unhurt Reyes, would mark all three boxes: Incapacitated and Dying, with the end of the scene as the deadline for a medic. Spending a Duty point when the disruptor hits reduces it to two boxes, leaving Reyes Seriously wounded but still standing. If Reyes is already down at three boxes, another disruptor shot or a lirpa blow kills; a punch does not.

To take someone down without hurting them, use a phaser on stun, a grapple, or the Vulcan nerve pinch rather than a weapon from this list.

## Phaser settings

| Setting | Effect on a hit |
|---|---|
| Stun | Target rolls Body at −2. Failure incapacitates them for the scene; success gives −2 on their next action roll. Stun marks no injury boxes. |
| Heat or cut | Controlled tool use. Material, time, and the task determine the skill and difficulty. |
| Kill | Marks three injury boxes: the target is Incapacitated and Dying. Disintegration requires a deliberate setting and an exposed target; the GM determines whether it is possible. |

Declare the setting before firing. Stun effects do not stack; a later failed resistance roll still incapacitates the target. Unarmed strikes normally mark one box. A grapple uses an opposed Unarmed Combat contest; winning restrains the target while the hold is maintained. Escape uses an action and a new contest.

## Phaser power

Hand phasers run on power cells, and every shot draws on them. This is a campaign rule; the GM tracks opponents' weapons loosely.

| Phaser | Power |
|---|---|
| Type 1 hand phaser | 8 |
| Type 2 phaser pistol | 16 |
| Phaser rifle | 30 |

| Setting | Power per use |
|---|---|
| Stun | 1 |
| Heat or cut | 1 per use as a tool |
| Kill | 2 |
| Wide-beam stun (everyone in a small area; each target makes the stun roll) | 3 |
| Disintegrate (when the GM allows it) | 4 |

- **Firing spends power whether the shot hits or misses.** A Duty point reroll repeats the same shot and costs nothing extra.
- **A drained phaser won't fire.** Swap in a spare power cell (one action) to refill it, or recharge it fully in the ship's armory between missions. Having packed a spare cell is a good use of a Duty point's *small preparation*.
- **Overload.** A phaser with at least 4 power left can be set to overload: it whines for a round and explodes at the end of the next round, as an explosion (1 box to everyone nearby, 2 at the centre). The phaser is destroyed.

## The battle map

The GM view of the creator has a **battle map** for fights where it helps to see who is where. It is a tool, not a rule: everything on it follows the rules in this guide, and a fight without it works the same way. Open **How to use the map** on the map itself for the buttons, step by step.

**Three kinds of map.**

- **A hex map** for an away team's fight where position matters: cover, doorways, who can reach whom. The GM chooses what one hex is: 1 yard inside a ship, 5 or 10 yards on a city street, 50 on open ground, or a number of kilometres for ships in space. Dragging a token or measuring shows the distance and its **range band**, so the GM can read the penalty from the Range tables.
- **A ship fight** on a range track: a lane for the Pelion and one for each space range band, from under 10 km to a light-second. When the helms make their contest, the GM moves the enemy a lane in or out. **Add the Pelion** places the ship, linked to its Shields, Hull and systems: marking a box on the map marks it on the ship's tracks, and the other way round. Enemy ships come from the *Enemy ships* table with their Hull boxes.
- **A range track** for an away team's chase or a running fight where only distance matters.

**Tokens.** The GM places the crew with one button. Their tokens stay linked to the officers in the GM view: marking Injury, Dying, Stunned or a Duty point on a token marks it on the officer, and the other way round. Opponents come from the Threats table with their boxes filled in. Their pips fill as they are hurt, and a token whose boxes are all marked is taken out.

**Keeping a map.** The map stays in the GM's browser. **Save map to file** keeps every map, its picture and tokens in one file, to prepare a fight ahead of time or share it with another GM.

# Medical care

Starfleet physicians in 2268 have advanced diagnostic scanners, potent pharmaceuticals, portable emergency equipment, and sophisticated sickbay facilities. Familiar injuries often respond quickly to treatment. Severe trauma, incompatible biology, missing supplies, and unidentified conditions still challenge an experienced doctor. The treatment values below are campaign rules inspired by the series, rather than numerical rules stated in canon.

## Diagnosis and stabilization

A medical scanner identifies ordinary injuries automatically. Roll Medicine when the diagnosis is uncertain, physiology is unfamiliar, or an urgent treatment decision has meaningful consequences. Routine established treatments also succeed automatically when qualified staff have sufficient time and facilities.

Emergency stabilization takes one action and a successful Medicine roll with a medical kit. It prevents deterioration from the treated injury while the patient is protected from further harm, and it ends Dying. Stabilization alone removes no injury boxes. A patient at three boxes remains incapacitated until treatment clears a box.

## Field treatment

With a medical kit and several uninterrupted minutes, successful Medicine removes one injury box. Success by 5 or more removes two. Each injury receives one field-treatment attempt, even if that attempt fails; further attempts require changed circumstances such as better equipment, new diagnostic information, or access to sickbay. New injuries can receive their own treatment, but cannot justify treating an old injury repeatedly.

## Sickbay treatment

A functioning, equipped sickbay grants +2 to Medicine for diagnosis and treatment. A successful treatment removes two injury boxes; success by 5 or more removes three. Allow the time the procedure requires rather than completing surgery in one combat action. Sickbay care can follow field treatment. Do not repeat an unsuccessful procedure without a meaningful change in the situation.

Removing injury boxes removes their associated penalties, but major organ damage, severe burns, and complex internal trauma can still require a procedure and recovery period set by the GM. The physician determines whether the patient is fit for duty. Pain suppression does not itself heal an injury. Stun normally wears off at the end of the scene; prolonged unconsciousness requires assessment.

## Natural recovery

Injuries also heal with time. Rest clears injury one box at a time, and the worse you're hurt, the longer each box takes. Rest means light duty at most: no combat, no away missions, nothing strenuous. A day spent working hard or fighting clears nothing.

| Boxes marked | Rest to clear one box |
|---|---|
| 1 | A full day |
| 2 | At least three days |
| 3 | A week, and only once treated |

- **At three boxes, rest alone clears nothing.** An Incapacitated patient needs stabilizing or treatment (field care or sickbay) first; after that, a week of rest clears the first box.
- **The times add up.** From three boxes, rest alone takes about a week and a half to heal completely: a week, then three days, then a day.
- **Rest and medicine combine.** Treatment clears boxes immediately; rest clears what remains.
- **Serious trauma takes longer.** For major organ damage, severe burns, or complex internal trauma, the GM can require a set recovery period before rest clears anything, and the ship's physician decides when the patient is fit for duty.

*Example:* Ensign Reyes ends a mission Seriously wounded (two boxes). Sickbay is busy with worse cases, so Reyes rests in quarters: three days clear the first box and one more day the second, and Reyes is back on duty on the fifth day.

## Alien medicine and limits

Unknown illness or physiology requires discovering the cause and a suitable treatment before boxes can be restored. Medicine can receive assistance from Life Sciences, Sensors, or Computers. Existing assistance limits apply. Missing essential equipment or incompatible supplies can prevent a procedure. Disintegration and other unsurvivable injuries cannot be reversed by an ordinary Medicine roll.

Crew of other species may need care suited to their biology. An Alien Physiology Specialist, or a physician with access to the right species data, keeps that care from becoming guesswork.

## Examples from the original series

*Journey to Babel:* McCoy performs complex surgery on Sarek during a ship crisis; compatible blood and postoperative care remain important. *The Devil in the Dark:* McCoy improvises a treatment suited to the Horta's unusual biology. *Miri:* diagnosing an unfamiliar disease and establishing a safe treatment require investigation. These stories support powerful medicine whose limits come from the medical problem and available resources.

Canon reference: StarTrek.com, "8 Times Leonard 'Bones' McCoy's Medical Knowledge Saved the Day." <https://www.startrek.com/news/mccoys-medical-knowledge-saved-the-day>

# Duty points and bridge duty

## Using Duty points

Begin each session with two Duty points; unused points do not carry into the next session. Spend no more than one point on a single roll or injury event. Duty points belong to your character and cannot be transferred.

| Spend one point to | Timing and limits |
|---|---|
| Reroll your own roll | Declare after seeing the result but before its consequences are resolved. Keep the second result, even if worse. |
| Reduce an injury by one box | Declare when a hit would mark boxes, before applying them. Does not prevent disintegration or another hazard that bypasses the injury track. |
| Shrug off a telling hit | Cancel the effect an attacker picked against you; you still take the boxes. |
| Establish a small preparation | Propose a plausible detail, such as having brought a field kit. GM approval is required; it cannot contradict established facts or solve the mission outright. |

Gain one point when one of your complications creates a meaningful problem you accept. This includes a companion complication from a species advantage. The GM offers or approves the award. You can decline the proposed complication without a penalty. Repeating a harmless habit does not earn points.

*Example:* your curiosity tempts you to remain beside an anomaly while the team withdraws. If you accept the resulting separation and danger, the GM awards one Duty point. The point does not remove that danger.

## Working on the bridge

Describe the effect you want before selecting a skill. Each exchange gives occupied stations a chance to contribute. Roll for consequential decisions and uncertain work; routine station operation needs no roll.

| Station | Skill and contribution |
|---|---|
| Captain | Orders each exchange: Command, Read the enemy, Hail or Rally (see The captain's orders) |
| Helm | Helm to evade, pursue, or gain position |
| Navigation | Navigation to plot a safe course or escape |
| Science | Sensors to identify threats, weaknesses, or anomalies |
| Engineering | Engineering to repair systems or redirect power |
| Communications | Communications to establish contact or overcome interference |
| Tactical | Phasers to fire ship weapons |

### The captain's orders

The captain, or whoever has the conn, takes one of these each exchange:

| Order | Roll | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Command | Leadership | Success: one station gets +2 to its roll this exchange; success by 5, two stations. |
| Read the enemy | Psychology | Success: you predict their next move. +2 to the pilot's evade or range contest this exchange, or the gunner ignores one band of range penalty. |
| Hail | Diplomacy (a bluff included) | Opposed by the enemy captain's Will. Success: they pause to talk, hold fire for an exchange, or consider surrender; a bluff can make them believe what you say about your ship. |
| Rally | Leadership | After a hit to the hull, steady the crew: anyone stunned or shaken by the hit acts normally again, and the next failed station roll this scene may be rerolled. |

A captain who takes an order doesn't also work a station that exchange. Commanding well is the job.

## The USS Pelion

The Pelion is a **Heavy Cruiser**, and it has its own sheet, tracked like an injury track. The GM states what is at stake before the rolls and narrates what each box means. Every Starfleet class, and the ships the Pelion meets, is in the **Ship registry** below, adapted from Tiny Trek.

| Track | Boxes | Effect as boxes fill |
|---|---|---|
| Shields | 5 | While the shields are up, hits mark Shields first. With all 5 marked they're down, and hits go to the Hull. |
| Hull | 5 | 1–2: scarred, minor breaches sealed. 3–4: serious breaches, decks lost. 5: the ship is breaking up; start an *Abandon ship* clock. |
| Weapons, Engines, Sensors, Communications, Transporters, Life support | 3 each | 1 **Damaged**: −2 to rolls using it. 2 **Crippled**: −4, and it can do only its most basic job. 3 **Offline**: it doesn't work until repaired. |

**Power.** Each exchange the ship has **Power** to spend (the Pelion has 5), and it refills at the start of the next. Raising the shields for the exchange costs 1, firing the phasers 1, a torpedo volley 1 (and one torpedo from the load), and a transporter use 1. Each Power left over can be diverted for +1 to one ship roll that exchange, up to +2. With the shields not raised, hits go straight to the Hull.

**Taking hits.** A hit marks the boxes its weapon deals (see the registry): Shields first while they're up, then the Hull. **Every hit that reaches the Hull** also rolls on the *Concussive system damage* table below. A called shot or a telling hit can pick a system instead.

**Defending.** When an enemy ship fires, Helm may roll **Helm** to evade, once per exchange, like Dodge: success cancels the hit. Torpedoes can't be evaded. Engines damage applies its penalty.

**Maneuverability.** Compare the ships' Maneuverability: the most maneuverable ship adds the difference to its Helm contests and its evade rolls. If two ships tie for the highest, nobody gets the bonus.

**Repairs.** In the middle of a crisis, a successful **Engineering** roll clears one box from Shields or a system (two on success by 5), or undoes one concussive result; it takes the engineer's action that exchange. The Hull can only be patched by one box per scene, and fully repaired at a starbase. Shields that went down take an hour per box to restore.

**Firing.** Tactical rolls **Phasers** for ship weapons, against the enemy ship's own tracks, which the GM keeps the same way.

## Advancement

Crew members grow by earning **advancement points** and spending them.

- **Earning.** After a significant mission, the GM awards each crew member **1 point**. At a **milestone**, the end of a story arc, everyone earns **2 more**.
- **Spending.** Spend points when you earn them, or bank them for something bigger. Each purchase needs the GM's approval and a reason from the story: training at a starbase, something you lived through, a mentor aboard.

| Purchase | Cost | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| A skill from Untrained to Trained | 1 | — |
| A skill from Trained to Specialty | 2 | Specialty (+3) is the ceiling: only a General Services character's starting skill is Expert, unless they are commissioned (see Step 3) |
| Revise an advantage or complication | 1 | — |
| A second advantage | 3 | Once; a general advantage you don't have. Only one advantage's +2 counts on a roll |
| +1 to an attribute | 4 | Once per attribute; no higher than one above the starting maximum (14, plus any species bonus) |

Species changes still need a separate agreement with the GM. The creator keeps the ledger: award points in the advancement box under the skills (or from the Captain's log), then spend them there.

## Captain's log

End each mission with a short log, written together:

1. **The mission.** What were we sent to do, and did we do it: completed, partly, or failed?
2. **Who stood out?** Name a commendation, or a moment worth remembering.
3. **Complications.** Which ones came into play, and what did they cost?
4. **What changed?** Damage to the ship, new allies or enemies, open threads for next time.
5. **Advancement.** After a significant mission, the GM may authorize one skill step for each crew member (Untrained to Trained, or Trained to Specialty), or a revision of an advantage or complication.

The creator's GM view keeps the log, applies each crew member's advancement to the GM's copy, and saves both with the crew file. Players record an approved advancement on their own sheet under Step 3, so their character still passes the checklist.

# Species advantages

A species advantage **replaces** your general advantage. Each one comes with a **companion complication** that you take along with it. The companion works like any other complication: the GM proposes a concrete problem, and you gain a Duty point if you accept it. You keep your regular complication as well.

Most species advantages give +2 in one narrow situation, following the same rules as general advantages. A few are abilities instead (Vulcan Mind Meld, Aenar Telepathy, and Flight): they name the roll to make, and the GM sets the limits before the roll. They give no +2.

A custom species advantage follows the same pattern: a narrow +2 or an ability with agreed limits, paired with a companion complication that has a clear trigger and a real consequence.

# Making your own

The departments and species in this book are a starting point, not a limit. With the GM's agreement, any player can create their own department or species. The creator has a **Custom** option at the end of each list, and its checklist holds a custom choice to the same rules as everything else.

## A custom department

A department is a concept, not a package of powers: it gives no bonus of its own. A custom one only needs a clear place in the group.

1. **Name it and describe it.** One sentence for what your character does that no one else does, in the style of the departments above: *"Stellar Cartography: maps the unmapped, and knows which charts are lies."*
2. **Choose a group.** Pick the one it belongs with: Command (gold), Sciences (blue) or Operations (red). The group only sets your sheet's colors and emblem.
3. **Pick four core skills.** The skills it can't do without. Mark them Trained yourself in step 3 (the built-in departments do this for you); they count toward your normal Trained skills.
4. **Check it with the GM.** It should fit the setting and overlap the existing departments as little as possible. A custom department uses the officer training (a Specialty and nine Trained); enlisted crew belong to General Services.

**In the creator:** in step 1, choose **Custom department…** at the end of the Department list, then enter its name and group. Write the description in *What you bring*.

## A custom species

Build it with the GM, one step at a time.

### 1. The concept

Two or three sentences: where they come from, what sets them apart, and how others see them. It should be something the species in this book don't already cover.

### 2. Attribute adjustments

Give adjustments from **−2 to +2** per attribute that **total zero**: a +2 needs a −2 or two −1s. The adjustments move the attribute's base (+1 starts it at 11) and raise its starting maximum (13 plus the bonus). Most species change two to four attributes.

### 3. Core rules

One to three short rules that always apply: what they can do (see in the dark, resist cold, a free Trained skill) and what it costs (a weakness, a people's distrust, a hazard they can't abide). A strength with no drawback is worth more than it looks.

### 4. Species advantages

Write one to three. Each works like a general advantage: **+2 to one skill in a narrow situation**, or a small, limited permission ("once per scene…"). Each comes with a **companion complication**: a problem the trait causes, with a clear trigger and a real consequence, which earns a Duty point when it bites, like any complication. A character takes one advantage, general or species, so make them distinct choices.

### In the creator

1. In step 2, choose **Custom species…** at the end of the Species list.
2. Name it and set its adjustments. The checklist confirms they total zero.
3. Write its core rules in **Species ability (GM approval required)** in step 4.
4. In step 4, choose **Custom species advantage…** and enter the advantage and its companion complication.

### Example

**Kethari.** Tall, silver-scaled people from a high-gravity ocean world, newly allied with the Federation. *Adjustments:* Body +1, Agility −1. *Core rule:* high-gravity muscles: +1 to Athletics in normal gravity, but cold slows them (−1 to all rolls in freezing conditions). *Species advantage:* **Deep-Diver**: +2 to Athletics to swim and hold your breath. *Companion:* **Homesick for the Sea**: dry, cramped ships wear on you; when it matters, accept the short temper or the mistake.

# Complications

Choose one complication. These are sources of meaningful choices, not constant penalties. The GM proposes a concrete problem and offers one Duty point if you accept it. You may decline without penalty. A personality description, harmless habit, or repeated inconvenience earns no point by itself. Agree on boundaries before a complication affects another player's character.

Every complication needs a trigger: the concrete situation that brings it into play. The creator suggests three for each complication below; pick one, combine them, or write your own with the GM.

| Complication | What it does |
|---|---|
| Cannot Leave a Mystery Alone | An unexplained reading or strange discovery tempts you to delay departure, spend a scarce resource, or pursue a risky investigation. Accept a specific cost rather than receiving points merely for asking questions. |
| Protects Others at Personal Risk | You can shield someone, stay behind, or volunteer for a hazardous rescue at a real cost to yourself. This does not automatically prevent their injury. |
| Duty Before Self | You prioritize your assignment despite exhaustion, injury, or a conflicting personal need. Accept a concrete setback; ordinary professionalism earns no point. |
| Conscience Above Orders | A particular order conflicts with your ethical convictions. Accept a meaningful delay, formal challenge, or consequence of refusing it. The complication does not determine what your captain orders. |
| By the Book | You insist on an established procedure when improvisation would be faster. Accept a genuine loss of time or opportunity, rather than penalizing every roll. |
| Distrusts Command Decisions | A troubling order makes you demand clarification or independent verification at a critical moment. Agree on the resulting delay or strain; it is not permission to disrupt every scene. |
| Reputation to Uphold | Pride in your record tempts you to conceal uncertainty, refuse help, or accept a task beyond your readiness. Take a real consequence when that choice matters. |
| Haunted by a Previous Mission | A specific reminder of an earlier loss or disaster causes hesitation, distraction, or a difficult choice. Define the reminder and effect together; avoid a penalty whenever danger appears. |
| Uneasy Around Telepathy | You hesitate to cooperate with a mind meld or another mental procedure, complicating a situation where it could help. This grants neither mental resistance nor knowledge of someone's intentions. |
| Cultural Friction | An established custom or expectation clashes with a local practice. Accept a misunderstanding, difficult negotiation, or lost opportunity. Species identity alone is not a complication. |
| Old Rival | An agreed recurring rival challenges your credibility or complicates your assignment. The rival's intervention must create a concrete problem, not merely an unpleasant conversation. |
| Someone Back Home | A personal obligation or concern conflicts with mission demands. Establish who matters to you and what kinds of conflict are welcome before using this complication. |
| Medical Restriction | An agreed condition or treatment requirement becomes relevant under particular circumstances. Define its trigger and consequence; it does not impose a blanket penalty or entitle the GM to invent new symptoms. |
| Experimental Enthusiasm | You are tempted to use an unproven technique before adequate testing. Accept a specific risk, resource cost, or troublesome side effect agreed before attempting it. |
| Keeps a Dangerous Secret | An agreed secret threatens trust, reputation, or an assignment. Earn a point when protecting or revealing it creates an actual consequence. Establish the secret with the GM rather than inventing it to avoid a problem. |
| Reluctant to Use Lethal Force | You seek a nonlethal approach even when it costs time or tactical position. Ordinary Starfleet restraint earns no point; there must be a meaningful additional difficulty. |

## Using and revising traits

General advantages and complications describe individual crew members and can suit any species when appropriate. A Vulcan might choose Mental Discipline as a general advantage; species advantages are the only traits tied to a species. At a mission milestone, revise a trait when events change the character. Custom advantages follow the same narrow +2 benefit; custom complications need a clear trigger and a meaningful, accepted consequence.

# Quick reference

## Will and mental resistance

Roll against Will to resist overwhelming fear, alien compulsion, or mental intrusion, or to maintain concentration through severe distraction. Apply the normal difficulty modifiers. Ordinary danger does not require a Will roll. If an adversary actively imposes mental influence, use an opposed contest of your Will against the adversary's relevant ability.

Before the roll, the GM states the possible effect and its duration. Failed fear or concentration rolls normally cause brief hesitation or −2 on your next action, rather than forcing a belief or personal decision. Mental intrusion can impose a specific effect established before the roll; resisting that effect does not reveal the intruder's identity or intentions.

Will protects your resolve; Presence influences others. Leadership and Diplomacy still use Presence. Species adjustments may change your starting Will, but mental advantages apply only to their agreed, narrow circumstances.

## At the table

1. State your goal and approach.
2. Choose the skill and apply modifiers.
3. Resolve one assistant's roll if applicable.
4. Roll 3d6 at or below the adjusted skill.
5. Apply the result and describe what changes.

## Summary

**Creation:** attributes start at 10, adjusted by species (always totaling zero), plus 5 points; maximum 13 plus any species bonus. One Specialty and nine Trained skills (General Services: one Expert at +5 and seven Trained). One advantage (general or species) and one complication; a species advantage brings its companion complication.

**Skills:** Untrained = attribute −4; Trained = attribute +1; Specialty = attribute +3; Expert (General Services only) = attribute +5.

**Modifiers:** favorable +2; ordinary 0; difficult −2; very difficult −4; extreme −6. Natural 3–4 succeeds; natural 17–18 fails. Margin = adjusted skill − roll.

**Assistance:** success +1; success by 5 or more +2; failure no bonus; 18 causes a complication. One assistant per roll.

**Combat:** short movement plus one action. Successful attack → optional Dodge → weapon effect. Dodge = Agility −2, plus Athletics training (+0 Untrained, +1 Trained, +2 Specialty), once per round. One injury box gives −1 to physical actions; two give −2; three incapacitate.

**Damage:** the weapon decides. Fists, clubs, knives, arrows and primitive firearms mark 1 box; heavy blades mark 2; a phaser or disruptor on kill marks 3 and leaves the target Dying (dead at the end of the scene unless stabilized); stun marks none. At three boxes, another hit from a 2-box or killing weapon is fatal.

**Near misses:** fail by 1–2 and the GM may offer success at a cost; never on a natural 17–18.

**Clocks:** 4, 6 or 8 segments. Danger clocks fill on failures and time; progress clocks fill on successes (2 segments on success by 5).

**Phaser power:** Type 1 holds 8, Type 2 16, rifle 30. Stun 1, heat/cut 1, kill 2, wide-beam stun 3, disintegrate 4. Spare cell refills; overload needs 4+.

**Ship:** Shields (3) take hits first; then Hull (4) and a system (3 each: −2, −4, offline). Normal hit 1 box, heavy hit 2. Helm evades once per exchange; Engineering clears a box.

**Medicine:** one action to stabilize. Field care takes several minutes and clears one box, or two on success by 5. Sickbay grants +2 and clears two boxes, or three on success by 5. Major trauma may require further recovery. Rest clears one box per full day, but not from three boxes: treatment must clear the first one.

**Will:** resist overwhelming fear or mental intrusion and maintain concentration. Ordinary danger needs no roll. Fear or distraction failure normally causes hesitation or −2 on your next action.

**Duty points:** start each session with two. Spend one to reroll, reduce an injury by one box, or establish a plausible preparation. Earn one when an accepted complication, including a companion complication, creates a meaningful problem.
