                  Legend Dynamics · Gothic Western

                  Trails of Blood & Dust Players' Guide

                  A Gothic Western for the Legend Dynamics RPG systemBy Don Smallwood

                  The railroad is coming, and it is bringing more than settlers. In the fog-bound timber towns and lamplit mining camps, the old things of the world have followed the wagons west: wolves that walk as men, the dead that will not stay buried, pale strangers who only come out after dark, and witches who know their names. Trails of Blood & Dust is a Gothic Western for the Legend Dynamics RPG system. Most people never see the darkness. You do, and you stand between it and them.

                  Trails of Blood & Dust is not tied to a single place or year. Set your game in any territory, from the 1860s to the 1890s.

                  The rule to remember

                  Roll 3d6 at or below your adjusted skill to succeed. Your skill already includes your training and attribute. Do not add the attribute again.

                  Example: a skill of 13, with a −2 penalty for bad conditions, gives a target of 11. A roll of 9 succeeds by 2. A roll of 12 fails by 1.

                  How this book is organized

                  This book combines the Legend Dynamics core rules, which every setting shares, with the Trails of Blood & Dust setting: its callings, origins, skills, gear, weapons and dangers. In this setting, the core rules’ Legend points are called Grit points. The material for running the game (job seeds, the monsters’ weaknesses and curses, the threats, and the GM’s tools) is in a companion book, the GM’s Guide.

                  Create your character

                  Work through the five steps in order. The character creator follows the same steps and checks the rules for you.

                  Step 3: Choose trained skills

                  A Well-rounded character chooses ten different skills: one Specialty at its governing attribute +3 and nine Trained skills at attribute +1. An Old hand chooses one Expert skill at +5 and seven Trained skills at +1. Each Extra Trained skill Edge adds one more Trained skill. All other skills are Untrained at attribute −4 when an attempt is plausible. Each skill has its own final target number; do not add the attribute again when rolling.

                  The Expert’s extra bonus matters most when the work is hard. With an attribute of 13, an Expert has a target of 18, which only natural 17–18 can fail; under a −4 “very difficult” penalty the target is still 14. If Athletics is the Expert skill, Dodge adds +2, the same as a Specialty.

                  Some origins give one skill Trained for free; it doesn’t use one of your Trained picks.

                  Broadening. An Old hand can broaden later (the GM’s call, usually after a significant job): the Expert skill becomes a Specialty at +3, and two more skills become Trained.

                  Step 4: Describe your character

                  Choose your name, title if any, and a short reason for what you do. Then choose one advantage and one complication.

                  Top 10 reasons

                  1. Justice. Somebody has to stand up for the people who can’t.
                  2. Vengeance. Someone did me wrong, and I mean to settle it.
                  3. Fortune. There’s money out here for anyone with nerve enough to take it.
                  4. A fresh start. Nobody out here knows what I did back east.
                  5. Duty. I took an oath, and I keep my word.
                  6. Freedom. No boss, no fences, no one to answer to.
                  7. Family. Everything I do is for my family.
                  8. Faith. The Lord sent me here for a reason, and I believe this is it.
                  9. Redemption. I did harm once. I mean to balance the ledger.
                  10. Nowhere else to go. These people took me in when no one else would.
                  • Advantage. Choose either a general advantage or one of your origin’s advantages. An origin advantage replaces the general advantage and always brings its companion complication.
                  • Complication. Choose one complication. You keep it even if you take an origin advantage, so a character with an origin advantage has two complications.
                  • Allegiance (optional). Choose an allegiance below, or none.

                  An advantage gives +2 to one narrowly defined kind of task when the GM agrees it applies. It cannot improve every use of a broad skill. A complication creates choices and problems; it is not a permanent penalty.

                  Actions and teamwork

                  When to roll

                  Routine work succeeds automatically when you have appropriate training, gear and time. Roll when failure could change the situation. Before rolling, establish your goal, the relevant skill, any modifiers, and what is at stake.

                  Circumstances Modifier
                  Favorable conditions or excellent preparation +2
                  Ordinary challenge under pressure 0
                  Difficult −2
                  Very difficult −4
                  Extreme −6

                  Apply the main circumstance modifier once. Add separately specified effects such as assistance or injury when they apply. The GM avoids charging twice for the same obstacle.

                  Results and margins

                  Add the three dice. A total at or below the adjusted skill succeeds. Margin of success equals the adjusted skill minus the roll. A larger margin may mean better information, more precise work, or a stronger position.

                  A natural 3 or 4 always succeeds. A natural 17 or 18 always fails. An impossible action receives no roll.

                  In an opposed contest, each side rolls its relevant skill. One success beats one failure. If both succeed, the larger success margin wins. A tie, or two failures, favors the defender or preserves the situation. For an automatic success above its target, count its contest margin as zero.

                  Assistance

                  One qualified assistant can roll a relevant skill before the lead character rolls. Success grants +1 to the lead roll; success by 5 or more grants +2. Failure gives no bonus. An 18 introduces a complication described by the GM. Other characters can do other tasks but cannot stack assistance bonuses on the same roll.

                  Failure and another attempt

                  Failure changes the situation: time passes, the trail goes cold, supplies run out, or danger grows. Do not repeat an unchanged roll until it succeeds. A new approach, new evidence, better tools, or more time may justify another attempt.

                  Success at a cost

                  When a roll fails by only 1 or 2, the GM may offer success at a cost: you get what you were after, but something else goes wrong. Typical costs are an injury box, broken gear, lost time (tick a clock), a box on a track, or someone noticing. The GM names the cost before you accept; you can always take the plain failure instead. A natural 17 or 18 is never a success at a cost.

                  Progress clocks

                  A clock is a circle cut into 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments that tracks something that takes time or builds toward disaster.

                  • Danger clocks fill when things go wrong: a failed roll, a success at a cost, or time passing. When one fills, the danger happens.
                  • Progress clocks fill as the characters succeed: one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. When one fills, the goal is achieved.
                  • The GM sizes the clock (2 = quick, 4 = urgent or simple, 6 = substantial, 8 = long or complex) and shows it to the players.

                  Investigation and social scenes

                  Ask concrete questions and explain your approach. Evidence may require several different tasks. A successful roll does not force someone to abandon a core belief or reveal what they do not know.

                  Discovery clocks and lore cards

                  When a job turns on an unknown subject (a supernatural creature and its weakness), the GM sets up a discovery clock. It fills in three phases, and filling it mints a lore card: what the characters learned, kept for good. A character’s lore cards are their hunter’s almanac.

                  Phase What it means
                  1. Confront the unknown Effects without a cause: a body drained white, cattle torn open, a town gone too quiet. You may suspect, but nothing is confirmed.
                  2. Identify Name the creature. This is the gate: until its identity is confirmed, no lore card applies, and the GM treats your theories as suspicion only.
                  3. Uncover Learn how it is beaten. Filling this phase fills the clock and mints a lore card.
                  • Sizing. The GM decides how many segments each phase needs (usually 1–2 to Confront, 2–3 to Identify, 2–3 to Uncover) and which methods can advance it, and shows the clock to the players.
                  • Advancing. Each roll that advances the clock uses a concrete method tied to a skill (below): one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. The clock stops at the end of Identify until the subject is confirmed: segments never spill past the gate.
                  • The primary investigator. One character leads and owns the lore card. Others assist under the usual assistance rules, rolling a relevant skill. A character who holds a relevant lore card can assist with a method they couldn’t otherwise bring (the lore card is why they’re qualified); it adds nothing beyond the normal assistance.
                  • Pushing. A player can push to dig faster or harder: a success advances one extra segment, but a track ticks (below) whatever the roll.

                  The lore card

                  A lore card grants no bonus to any roll. It is an earned permission: knowledge that makes possible an action otherwise forbidden or unavailable against that subject. Without it, the GM can rule that the action isn’t possible: you can’t aim for a heart you don’t know is the weakness, or speak a name you never learned. A lore card can authorize a called shot or a telling-hit effect that would otherwise be unavailable.

                  • A lore card never helps confirm its own subject: holding one doesn’t prove this is that. It applies once the Identify phase has confirmed it.
                  • Shared copies. Every character present when the lore card is minted (the climactic confrontation or the breakthrough) gets a shared copy; the primary investigator is recorded as its owner. An absent character doesn’t, though a holder can teach it to them later with the GM’s agreement.
                  • What it records. The subject (its tag), what was learned, what it permits, the job where it was earned, its owner, and who was there. It’s kept on the character sheet, under Learned in play.

                  The Harvest

                  A discovery clock always races a threat clock. While you dig, the creature is still hunting. The Harvest clock (4 or 6 segments, one per job) ticks once for each scene spent investigating and once for each failed investigation roll. Each time it fills, the creature claims another victim, and the clock starts again. The dread is the race: learn its weakness before it takes someone you were protecting.

                  What investigating costs

                  Chases

                  Run a chase as a progress clock for the pursuers against a danger clock for the quarry’s escape. Each exchange, the characters roll the skill that moves them (Riding, or Athletics on foot): success ticks the pursuers’ clock, failure ticks the escape. Terrain, a tired mount or a damaged engine, and attacks made on the move (at −2) adjust the rolls.

                  Personal combat

                  Turn order and actions

                  Act in descending Agility order. Players choose the order of tied characters; the GM resolves other ties. A round lasts a few seconds. On your turn, move a short distance (up to 10 yards: the Close band below) and take one meaningful action, such as attacking, reloading, helping, or attempting a task.

                  Attacking and defending

                  Roll Shooting for a ranged attack, Melee for a weapon in hand, or Brawling for fists and grappling. If the attack succeeds, an aware target able to move may defend: Dodge, Parry or Block. A successful defense cancels the hit. Apply injury penalties to every defense.

                  • One defense a round. A target gets one defense a round, between its turns, and chooses which when an attack hits. The Additional Defense Edge gives a second.
                  • Dodge is Agility −2, with +1 for Trained Athletics or +2 for Athletics as a Specialty or Expert.
                  • Parry. Against a weapon in hand or fists, roll Melee −3 with a weapon in hand, or Brawling −3 bare-handed against fists. An open hand can’t parry a blade, claws or fangs. A second parry in the same round (with Additional Defense) is a further −2. Nobody parries a shot or a missile.
                  • Block. With a shield, or anything that can stand in for one (a chair, a plank, a crate lid), you can block a weapon in hand, fists, or a thrown or loosed missile: roll Melee −2, plus the shield’s bonus. Nothing you can lift blocks a gunshot or a blast. An improvised shield breaks the first time it blocks a hit of 2 or more boxes.
                  • Attack quality. A clean hit is harder to escape. When an attack succeeds by 3 or 4, the defense is at −1; by 5 or 6, −2; by 7 or more, −3.
                  • Aim. Spend your action aiming at a target you can see: +1 to your next attack against it. An advantage that rewards aiming adds its bonus to this.

                  Solid cover blocks a shot. Partial cover imposes −2 on the attack. Weapon effects apply after a hit survives any defense. Success margin does not increase damage: a great hit decides what happens instead.

                  Telling hits

                  A really good shot, or a blow that lands exactly where it was meant to, lets the attacker say what it does.

                  • A telling hit is an attack that succeeds by 5 or more, or on a natural 3 or 4, and isn’t stopped by a defense. The attacker picks one effect below and describes it. The weapon still marks its usual boxes.
                  • A called shot. Before rolling, name the effect you want and take −2 on the attack, or −4 for a small target (a hand, an eye, a rope, a lock). If the attack hits at all, the effect happens, whatever the margin. An advantage that rewards aiming cuts this penalty by 1.
                  • Both ways. Opponents land telling hits on characters too.
                  Effect What happens
                  Disarm The weapon or held item drops, or flies a few yards away
                  Knock down The target falls prone, or is driven back a few steps
                  Pin The target is held, tangled or pinned down: their next action goes to getting free
                  Rattle −2 to the target’s next roll
                  Break gear A shield, weapon or tool breaks; armor loses 1 protection until it’s repaired
                  Open them up The next attack on this target this round gets +2
                  Precise You hit exactly what you aimed at: the lantern, the rope, the tire, the hand on the trigger

                  Limits. A telling hit never adds boxes and never gets past a defense. The effect has to make sense: nobody disarms a bear or knocks down a train, and the GM can rule an effect out. Opponents who drop from one hit are already down, so a telling hit against them sets up what comes next, like sending one crashing into another.

                  Staying alive. A character on the receiving end can shrug off a telling hit’s effect with Hard to put down (once per fight) or by spending a Grit point. Either way, they still take the boxes.

                  Fighting in Trails of Blood & Dust

                  Trails of Blood & Dust adds these to the attack and defense rules above.

                  • Shields. A real shield is rare out West: a hide shield or an iron-bound buckler gives +1 to Block. Anything at hand stands in: a chair, a wooden slat, a crate lid, a saddle.
                  • Careful Marksman. Aiming with the advantage gives +3 in all: Aim’s +1 and the advantage’s +2.
                  • A heavy coat. A buffalo coat or a canvas duster turns a blade, a claw or an arrow, but not a bullet. Once per fight, a hit of 2 or more boxes from a blade, claw, fang or missile marks one fewer.

                  Example: a werewolf’s claws succeed by 5 against Deputy Ames, who hasn’t defended yet this round. Dodge is 11, −2 for the clean hit: Ames needs a 9 or less. A parry with the saber would be Melee 14 −3 −2 = 9, no better, so Ames dodges. When the second wolf lunges before Ames’s next turn, there’s no defense left, unless Ames has Additional Defense.

                  Stagecoach chases

                  A coach on the road with riders closing in is a chase where distance is everything. Run it on the battle map’s Stagecoach chase track, in the GM view, instead of the chase clocks.

                  • The lanes. The stagecoach has its own lane, and each rider stands in a range band from it: Close (alongside), Short, Medium, Long or Extreme. Riders usually start at Medium or Long.
                  • Each exchange. The driver rolls Riding against each rider’s Riding (11 for a threat without it). The winner moves that rider one band: closer if the rider won, farther if the driver did. A tie leaves it where it is, and a rider pushed past Extreme drops out of the chase. A fresh horse or a good road gives +2; rough ground or a tired team gives −2.
                  • Fighting on the move. Everyone else acts as usual. Shooting from the box, the roof or the saddle is at −2, on top of the range penalty. At Close, a rider can board with an Athletics roll, and then it is fists, knives and shotguns at arm’s length. A passenger can climb out to the box or onto the roof as their move.
                  • Hurting the coach. A hit aimed at the team or a wheel (a called shot, −2) marks one box on the coach’s track. Dynamite, a crash, or a driver who fails by 5 or more on rough ground marks one more.
                  • The driver goes down. If the driver is Incapacitated or knocked from the box, the team bolts. Someone must reach the box and take the reins (Riding −2) before the next exchange, or the coach marks a box.
                  Boxes The coach
                  0 Rolling: no damage
                  1 Holed: splinters and bullet holes; no penalty yet
                  2 A horse hit or a wheel cracked: −2 to the driver’s Riding
                  3 Lamed: −4 to the driver’s Riding, and riders close on a tie
                  4 Wrecked: the coach overturns or stops dead; everyone aboard rolls Athletics or marks a box of Injury

                  Injury

                  Boxes marked Condition Effect
                  0 Uninjured No injury penalty

                  Injury penalties replace each other rather than stacking. They apply to movement, attacks, Dodge, and other tasks the injury physically hinders.

                  Dying and death

                  • Dying. A hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes and brings you to three boxes leaves you Incapacitated and Dying. A Dying character dies at the end of the scene unless someone stabilizes them: one action and a successful Medicine roll with a doctor’s bag.
                  • A further hit. While you are at three boxes, any hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes kills you. Fists, clubs and knives just keep you down.
                  • Certain death. Some fates (a fall into a furnace, a beheading, disintegration) bypass the track entirely.

                  Your protection is avoiding the hit: a defense (Dodge, Parry or Block), cover, and a Grit point, which reduces a hit by one box.

                  • A character rolls 3d6 on the table below and suffers the result on top of the boxes.

                  Medical care

                  Frontier medicine has chloroform, carbolic acid, laudanum and steady hands, and not much else. Infection kills more men than bullets do, and no doctor’s bag holds a cure for a bite from something that should be dead. See Bites and curses below.

                  Diagnosis and stabilization

                  Roll Medicine when the diagnosis is uncertain or an urgent treatment decision matters. Emergency stabilization takes one action and a successful Medicine roll with a doctor’s bag. It ends Dying and stops deterioration, but removes no boxes.

                  Field treatment

                  With a doctor’s bag and several uninterrupted minutes, successful Medicine removes one injury box; success by 5 or more removes two. Each injury receives one field-treatment attempt; further attempts need changed circumstances.

                  Proper care

                  A doctor’s surgery with instruments, chloroform and a clean table grants +2 to Medicine. A successful treatment removes two boxes; success by 5 or more removes three. Allow the time the treatment requires.

                  Natural recovery

                  Rest clears injury one box at a time, and the worse you’re hurt, the longer each box takes. Rest means nothing strenuous: no fighting, no hard travel, no long days of work.

                  Boxes marked Rest to clear one box
                  1 A full day
                  2 At least three days
                  3 A week, and only in someone’s care

                  At three boxes, rest alone does nothing until someone has stabilized or treated you; after that, a week of rest in their care clears the first box. A successful treatment is faster. The times add up: from three boxes, rest alone takes about a week and a half to heal completely (a week, then three days, then a day).

                  Monsters and magic

                  The Sight

                  A few people are born Sighted. They see ghosts, the true faces of monsters wearing human ones, and magic at work. To everyone else the darkness is a rumor, until it isn’t.

                  Monstrous forms

                  Werewolves and vampires have a monstrous form: the wolf, or the feral face. Changing is a free action, but fury, terror or pain can force it (a Will roll), and a werewolf has no choice at moonrise under the full moon. While changed, the form’s profile applies: the creator’s In monstrous form box adds its bonuses to your rolls. Horses bolt (−2 to Riding), and a monstrous form seen by townsfolk marks a box of Whispers.

                  Making your own

                  The callings and origins in this book are a starting point, not a limit. With the GM’s agreement, any player can create their own calling or origin. The creator has a Custom option at the end of each list, and its checklist holds a custom choice to the same rules as everything else.

                  A custom calling

                  A calling is a concept, not a package of powers: it gives no bonus of its own. A custom one only needs a clear place in the group.

                  1. Name it and describe it. One sentence for what your character does that no one else does, in the style of the callings above: “Gravedigger: digs the holes, reads the stones, and knows which of the dead are restless.”
                  2. Choose a group. Pick the one it belongs with: The Law, The Learned or The Frontier. The group only sets your sheet’s colors and emblem.
                  3. Pick four core skills. The skills it can’t do without. Mark them Trained yourself in step 3 (the built-in callings do this for you); they count toward your normal Trained skills.
                  4. Check it with the GM. It should fit the setting and overlap the existing callings as little as possible.

                  In the creator: in step 1, choose Custom calling… at the end of the Calling list, then enter its name and group. Write the description in What you bring.

                  A custom origin

                  Build it with the GM, one step at a time.

                  1. The concept

                  Two or three sentences: where they come from, what sets them apart, and how others see them. It should be something the origins in this book don’t already cover.

                  2. The tier

                  The tier measures how much the origin gives on its own. Each tier costs one of your 3 Edge points, so stronger origins start with fewer Edges.

                  Tier Edges Use it for
                  0 3 Ordinary people: a homeland, upbringing or culture, with no special abilities
                  1 2 One small, constant edge: keen senses, a tough hide, a free Trained skill, resistance to one hazard
                  2 1 Clear inhuman or supernatural gifts: venom, a second form, strong resistances
                  3 0 Remarkable beings: several strong abilities, and serious weaknesses to match

                  When in doubt, choose the higher tier.

                  3. Attribute adjustments

                  Give adjustments from −2 to +2 per attribute that total zero: a +2 needs a −2 or two −1s. The adjustments move the attribute’s base (+1 starts it at 11) and raise its starting maximum (13 plus the bonus). Most origins change two to four attributes.

                  4. Core rules (tier 1 and up)

                  One to three short rules that always apply: what they can do (see in the dark, resist cold, a free Trained skill) and what it costs (a weakness, a people’s distrust, a hazard they can’t abide). A strength with no drawback is worth more than it looks, so price it a tier higher.

                  5. Origin advantages

                  Write one to three. Each works like a general advantage: +2 to one skill in a narrow situation, or a small, limited permission (“once per scene…”). Each comes with a companion complication: a problem the trait causes, with a clear trigger and a real consequence, which earns a Grit point when it bites, like any complication. A character takes one advantage, general or origin, so make them distinct choices.

                  6. An alternate form (optional, tier 2 or 3)

                  If the origin changes shape, give the form attribute bonuses (usually +2 to one attribute and +1 to another), two or three traits, and its drawbacks. The shared rules for forms in Origins apply.

                  In the creator

                  1. In step 2, choose Custom origin… at the end of the Origin list.
                  2. Name it, choose the tier you agreed, and set its adjustments. The checklist confirms they total zero.
                  3. Write its core rules in Other ability (GM approval required) in step 4.
                  4. In step 4, choose Custom origin advantage… and enter the advantage and its companion complication.

                  Ghost-touched (tier 1). You died once, for a minute, and came back with something following you. Adjustments: Will +1, Body −1. Core rule: the dead notice you: you can sense a ghost nearby, but ghosts can sense you too. Origin advantage: Speaks with the Dead: +2 to Occult to call up and question a ghost. Companion: Cold Company: something from the other side follows you; when it matters, accept the chill, the stopped clock or the panicked horse.

                  Quick reference

                  Creation: attributes start at 10, adjusted by origin (always totaling zero), plus 5 points; maximum 13 plus any origin bonus. 3 points split between origin tier and Edges. Well-rounded: one Specialty and nine Trained. Old hand: one Expert at +5 and seven Trained. One advantage (general or origin) and one complication; an optional Allegiance.

                  Skills: Untrained = attribute −4; Trained = attribute +1; Specialty = attribute +3; Expert (Old hand) = attribute +5.

                  Modifiers: favorable +2; ordinary 0; difficult −2; very difficult −4; extreme −6. Natural 3–4 succeeds; natural 17–18 fails.

                  Assistance: success +1; success by 5 or more +2; 18 causes a complication. One assistant per roll.

                  Near misses: fail by 1–2 and the GM may offer success at a cost; never on a natural 17–18.

                  Clocks: 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments. Danger clocks fill on failures and time; progress clocks fill on successes (2 segments on success by 5).

                  Combat: move plus one action. Hit → one defense a round (Dodge, Parry or Block; −1 to −3 against a clean hit) → weapon effect. Dodge = Agility −2 plus Athletics training (+0, +1, +2). Parry −3, Block −2 plus the shield. Aim: +1. Telling hit (by 5+ or natural 3–4): the attacker picks an effect; called shot −2 (small target −4). A 2-box weapon that brings you to three boxes leaves you Dying; at three boxes, another 2-box hit kills.

                  Medicine: stabilize in one action. Field care clears 1 box (2 on margin 5+). Proper care gives +2 and clears 2 (3 on margin 5+). Rest clears a box in a day at 1 box, three days at 2, a week at 3 (in someone’s care).

                  Grit: start each session with two. Reroll, reduce an injury by one box, shrug off a telling hit, or establish a small preparation. Earn one from an accepted complication.

                  Monsters

                  The details behind Monsters and magic in the Players’ Guide: what each monster fears, what a lore card for it permits, and what its bite or curse does. In the story, hunters learn these the hard way: a discovery clock, a survivor’s tale, or a fight that goes wrong.

                  Weaknesses

                  Every monster has a weakness, and finding it is often the whole job: a discovery clock that ends in a lore card (see Discovery clocks and lore cards in the Players’ Guide). The Monster Hunter advantage and the Sighted’s Monster’s Eye help with the Identify rolls. A weakness works whoever stumbles on it: silver marks its extra box even fired by someone who has never heard of werewolves. A lore card is what lets a hunter use it on purpose: a called shot at the heart, a line of salt laid in time, the true name spoken.

                  Bites and curses

                  • Zombie bite. A bite that marks a box starts a 4-segment Infection clock, ticking each hour. A successful Medicine roll with an Occult remedy stops it; when it fills, you die, and rise.
                  • Werewolf bite. A bite under the full moon can pass on the curse; the GM decides, and lifting it is a major ritual.
                  • Vampire bite. A vampire who drains a victim to Dying can choose to turn them.
                  • The wendigo’s hunger. It begins with someone who ate human flesh to survive a starving winter in the high passes. A character who does starts a 4-segment Hunger clock, ticking each week they go without it again; when it fills, they belong to the wendigo. Only a major ritual lifts it.
                  • A wraith’s touch. Someone killed by it rises as a wraith at the next new moon, unless they’re buried with salt in the coffin.
                  • A banshee’s keening. She is tied to a family’s blood, not a place: once she has keened for one of a line, she can follow that family from town to town.

                  The GM’s tools

                  The battle map

                  The GM view of the character creator has a battle map for fights where it helps to see who is where. It is a tool, not a rule: everything on it follows the Personal combat rules in the Players’ Guide, and a fight without it works the same way. Open How to use the map on the map itself for the buttons, step by step.

                  Two kinds of map.

                  • A hex map for fights where position matters: cover, doorways, who can reach whom. The GM chooses what one hex is: 1 yard for a brawl in a room, 5 or 10 yards for a street, 50 for open ground. Dragging a token or measuring shows the distance and its range band, so the GM can read the penalty from the Range table in the Players’ Guide.
                  • A range track for fights where only distance matters: a chase, a running fight across open country. It has a lane for the posse and one for each range band. Moving a token into the next lane moves it one band in or out.

                  Tokens. The GM places the posse with one button. Their tokens stay linked to the characters in the GM view: marking Injury, Dying, the stunned condition or a Grit point on a token marks it on the character, and the other way round. Opponents come from the setting’s threats with their boxes filled in. Their pips fill as they are hurt, and a token whose boxes are all marked is taken out.

                  Keeping a map. The map stays in the GM’s browser. Save map to file keeps both maps, their pictures and tokens in one file, to prepare a fight ahead of time or share it with another GM.

                  Stagecoach chase. Besides the plain range track, the map has a Stagecoach chase track. Add the stagecoach places the stagecoach in the first lane with its own Coach boxes; its panel shows what the damage does. The rules are under Stagecoach chases in the Players’ Guide.

                  The Encounter panel

                  The GM view also has an Encounter panel that runs a fight by the Personal combat rules and shows every roll. Add characters from the GM view and opponents from the Threats table (or make one up on the spot), then start: it keeps the turn order by Agility, and for each attack works out the range, the defense (Dodge, Parry or Block, one a round), the boxes marked and any telling hit, offering a Grit point choices at the moment they apply. Hits land on the character cards and the battle map, and with gritty combat on, grievous wounds are rolled and kept. The panel keeps a log and a summary of the fight, which can be saved to a file. It’s a helper, not a rule: the GM can rule any result differently, and the log says so.

                  The Discovery panel

                  The GM view’s Discovery panel runs discovery clocks and the Harvest they race. Start a clock with its segments per phase (or a plain progress clock for a repair or a task in the same race), and fill it as the investigation rolls succeed. It stops at the Identify gate until you name the subject; then it shows every lore card the characters in the GM view already hold for it. The Failed roll and Push buttons tick the track for how they were digging, and a failed roll ticks the Harvest too. When a clock fills, Mint gives the primary investigator the lore card and everyone you tick as present a shared copy. Save all to file afterwards, so the players’ character files carry it. The Discord bot’s /discovery and /cards commands do the same at a table that plays online.

                      Quick reference

                      Resolution
                      3d6 at or below adjusted skill. Natural 3–4 always succeeds; 17–18 always fails. Margin = target − roll.
                      Opposed
                      Success beats failure; both succeed → larger margin wins; ties and double failures favor the defender.
                      Combat
                      Move a short distance and take one action. Hit → target may defend once per round: Dodge, Parry or Block → weapon effect. Partial cover −2.
                      Defending
                      One defense a round (two with Additional Defense). Dodge: Agility −2 + Athletics. Parry a weapon in hand or fists: Melee or Brawling −3; a second parry −2 more. Block a weapon in hand, fists or a missile with a shield or anything that stands in for one: Melee −2 + the shield's bonus (never a gunshot or blast); an improvised one breaks on a 2+ box hit. Attack succeeded by 3–4: defense −1; 5–6: −2; 7+: −3. Aim (one action): +1 to your next attack on that target.
                      Telling hit
                      Hit by 5+ or a natural 3–4, not defended: the attacker picks one effect (disarm, knock down, pin, rattle −2, break gear, open them up +2, precise). Called shot: −2 (small target −4), and any hit gets the effect. No extra boxes. Shrug it off: Hard to put down (once a fight) or a point.
                      Range
                      Close 10 yd, Short 50 yd, Medium 150 yd, Long 500 yd, Extreme 1 mile. Within a weapon's effective band: no penalty; one band beyond −2, two −4, farther out of range. Move: Close; run (no action): Short.
                      Dying
                      A 2-box weapon that brings you to 3 boxes: Dying; dead at the end of the scene unless stabilized (one action, Medicine, doctor's bag). At 3 boxes, another 2-box hit kills.
                      Medicine
                      Stabilize: one action. Field care clears 1 box (2 on margin 5+). Proper care gives +2, clears 2 (3 on margin 5+).
                      Rest
                      Clearing one box: a full day at 1 box, at least three days at 2, a week at 3 (and only once someone has stabilized or treated you).
                      Grit
                      Reroll (keep second), reduce an injury by one box, shrug off a telling hit, or establish a small preparation.
                      Damage
                      Fists, clubs, knives, arrows: 1 box. Guns, heavy blades, claws: 2. Close-range shotgun or buffalo rifle: 3 and Dying.
                      Out West
                      A real shield: +1 to Block; a chair, slat, crate lid or saddle stands in. Heavy coat: once a fight, a 2+ box hit from a blade, claw, fang or missile marks one fewer.
                      Guns
                      Derringer 2, revolver 6, shotgun 2, rifle 15. Fan the hammer 3 (−2), both barrels 2 (+2 close), covering fire 3. Reload: one action. Natural 18 jams.
                      Monsters
                      Silver: werewolves. Stake, fire, sunlight, beheading: vampires. Headshot or fire: zombies. Holy water: undead and demons.
                      Magic
                      Rituals: 2-, 4- or 6-segment clock, Occult rolls at your tradition's pace. Charms: Trained 2, Specialty 3, Expert 4 (Sorcerer 1). Hexes (Sorcerer): one action; Specialty 1 Boost, Expert 2, −1 each.
