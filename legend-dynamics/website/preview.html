Legend Dynamics RPG system

Heroes are made of skill, nerve and a little luck.

A tabletop roleplaying system built on three six-sided dice and concrete skills. One core, many worlds: Gothic Westerns, starships, swords against sorcery, and superheroes under the law.

Legend Dynamics RPG system

Core rules

New to Legend Dynamics? Start here: you can make a character without reading these rules first. · Rules last updated 21 October 2026 · Changelog

Legend Dynamics is a roleplaying system for heroic stories in any genre: concrete skills, a 3d6 roll-under core, and a small pool of Legend points that reward characters for their flaws. These core rules are setting-neutral. A setting pack supplies the roles, origins, skills, gear, weapons, tracks and dangers of one genre, and renames a few terms to suit it.

The rule to remember

Roll 3d6 at or below your adjusted skill to succeed. Your skill already includes your training and attribute. Do not add the attribute again.

Example: a skill of 13, with a −2 penalty for bad conditions, gives a target of 11. A roll of 9 succeeds by 2. A roll of 12 fails by 1.

How this book is organized

These are the Legend Dynamics core rules, which every setting shares. A setting pack adds its roles, origins, skills, gear, weapons and dangers, and may rename terms: Legend points might be Grit in a Western or Resolve in space opera.

Create your character

Work through the five steps in order. The character creator follows the same steps and checks the rules for you.

Step 1: Choose your role

Choose a role and describe what you bring. Your role places you in a group, which only changes your sheet’s colors and seal. A title or rank is agreed with the GM and gives no bonus.

Your setting pack supplies this (roles).

The creator marks your role’s four core skills Trained when you choose it. Keep them, raise one to your Specialty, or swap any of them for other skills; changing role swaps the core skills you haven’t changed.

Specialist or Expert

Choose how your character trained (see Step 3):

  • Specialist: one Specialty at +3 and nine Trained skills.
  • Expert: one Expert skill at +5 and seven Trained skills: a lifetime spent at one thing.

Step 2: Choose your origin, spend Edges, assign attributes

Attributes

Attribute Used for
Body Strength, endurance, resisting injury
Agility Coordination, reflexes, movement
Intellect Reasoning, observation, knowledge
Presence Leadership, persuasion, social influence
Will Resolve, concentration, resisting fear and influence

Origin and tier

Choose your origin from the Origins chapter. Every attribute starts at 10, and your origin adjusts some of them; the result is your base. Adjustments always total zero.

Origins can differ in power, so each has a tier from 0 to 3. Every character has 3 points to split between origin power and Edges: the higher the tier, the fewer Edges. Edges are useful in every scene, which keeps characters of every tier equally worth playing.

Your setting pack supplies this (origin tiers).

Edges

Spend all your Edges. Each one buys:

Edge What you get Limit
+1 attribute point One more point to spend in the next step, within the normal maximum 2
Extra Trained skill One more Trained skill in Step 3 3
Second advantage A second general advantage, with no extra complication 1
Extra Legend Start every session with 3 Legend points instead of 2 1
A friend A named contact who helps once per mission 1
A fine weapon or tool An heirloom weapon, a master’s instruments or a fine kit: +2 to one narrowly defined use of a named skill (see Gear) 1
Additional Defense A second defense each round, at −2: Dodge, Parry or Block, in any mix (see Attacking and defending) 1
Hard to put down Once per fight, shrug off a telling hit’s effect: you still take its boxes (see Telling hits) 1

Each setting also offers special Edges of its own, such as powers in Aurora; its Players’ Guide lists them.

Spending points

After applying your origin, distribute five additional points among the attributes, plus one for each attribute Edge.

  • The starting maximum is 13, plus any origin bonus to that attribute.
  • You cannot lower an attribute below its base to gain points.

Custom origin

You can build your own origin with the GM. See Making your own for the steps.

Step 3: Choose trained skills

A Specialist character chooses ten different skills: one Specialty at its governing attribute +3 and nine Trained skills at attribute +1. An Expert chooses one Expert skill at +5 and seven Trained skills at +1. Each Extra Trained skill Edge adds one more Trained skill. All other skills are Untrained at attribute −4 when an attempt is plausible. Each skill has its own final target number; do not add the attribute again when rolling.

The Expert’s extra bonus matters most when the work is hard. With an attribute of 13, an Expert has a target of 18, which only natural 17–18 can fail; under a −4 “very difficult” penalty the target is still 14. If Athletics is the Expert skill, Dodge adds +2, the same as a Specialty.

Some origins give one skill Trained for free; it doesn’t use one of your Trained picks.

Broadening. An Expert can broaden later (the GM’s call, usually after a significant mission): the Expert skill becomes a Specialty at +3, and two more skills become Trained.

Step 4: Describe your character

Choose your name, title if any, and a short reason for what you do. Then choose one advantage and one complication.

Top 10 reasons

Your setting pack supplies this (reasons).

  • Advantage. Choose either a general advantage or one of your origin’s advantages. An origin advantage replaces the general advantage and always brings its companion complication.
  • Complication. Choose one complication. You keep it even if you take an origin advantage, so a character with an origin advantage has two complications.
  • Allegiance (optional). Choose an allegiance below, or none.

An advantage gives +2 to one narrowly defined kind of task when the GM agrees it applies. It cannot improve every use of a broad skill. A complication creates choices and problems; it is not a permanent penalty.

Allegiance

Anyone can owe allegiance to one of the setting’s powers. It’s a benefit paired with a burden; the burden works like a complication and earns Legend when it causes you a real problem.

The GM can add others met in play (the creator’s GM view has a Campaign allegiances list), and a player can write one with the GM: choose Another allegiance and give it a name, a benefit and a burden.

Your setting pack supplies this (allegiances).

Step 5: Prepare

Your Dodge is Agility −2, plus your Athletics training bonus: +0 if Untrained, +1 if Trained, or +2 if a Specialty or Expert. Your Move is 5 yards, +1 for every 2 points of Agility + Body above 20, plus the same Athletics bonus and any origin speed adjustment (see Move and reach). Begin uninjured, with two Legend points (three with Extra Legend) and your gear. Name your friend, or your fine weapon or tool and the one narrow use it improves, if you took those Edges. Skills and gear

Choose the skill that matches your method. The GM chooses one primary skill for each distinct task.

Your setting pack supplies this (skills).

Untrained actions

A plausible untrained attempt uses attribute −4. Specialized work such as surgery requires relevant training or direct guidance. Guidance permits an attempt only when the GM finds it credible; it does not grant the expert’s skill.

Gear

Gear makes tasks possible rather than easier. Ordinary gear grants no blanket bonus. Missing essential tools can make a task impossible, or very difficult (−4) when the GM finds an attempt plausible.

Fine weapons and tools

A fine weapon or tool grants +2 to one narrowly defined use of a named skill, agreed when chosen. It does not stack with an advantage bonus on the same roll and never improves Dodge, Parry, or Block.

The use is narrower than the skill: the item is fine at something. A use that would apply to almost every roll made with the item is too broad.

Fine item Narrow use (+2) Not covered
A rifle with a telescopic sight Aimed shots at Long range or farther Snap shots, anything at Close or Short
A watchmaker’s instruments Delicate repairs: clockwork, locks, small mechanisms Forcing, breaking or building anything large
A surgeon’s instruments Field treatment of a wound: stitching, dressing, setting Diagnosis, emergency stabilization
A great axe of old make Breaking a shield, a door or a weapon Ordinary attacks
Too broad: “my rifle: +2 to a ranged skill”, “my kit: +2 to Medicine”

When a roll qualifies for both the fine item and an advantage, take +2 once.

Your setting pack supplies this (gear).

Actions and teamwork

When to roll

Routine work succeeds automatically when you have appropriate training, gear and time. Roll when failure could change the situation. Before rolling, establish your goal, the relevant skill, any modifiers, and what is at stake.

Circumstances Modifier
Favorable conditions or excellent preparation +2
Ordinary challenge under pressure 0
Difficult −2
Very difficult −4
Extreme −6

Apply the main circumstance modifier once. Add separately specified effects such as assistance or injury when they apply. The GM avoids charging twice for the same obstacle.

Results and margins

Add the three dice. A total at or below the adjusted skill succeeds. Margin of success equals the adjusted skill minus the roll. A larger margin may mean better information, more precise work, or a stronger position.

A natural 3 or 4 always succeeds. A natural 17 or 18 always fails. An impossible action receives no roll.

In an opposed contest, each side rolls its relevant skill. One success beats one failure. If both succeed, the larger success margin wins. A tie, or two failures, favors the defender or preserves the situation. For an automatic success above its target, count its contest margin as zero.

Assistance

One qualified assistant can roll a relevant skill before the lead character rolls. Success grants +1 to the lead roll; success by 5 or more grants +2. Failure gives no bonus. An 18 introduces a complication described by the GM. Other characters can do other tasks but cannot stack assistance bonuses on the same roll.

Failure and another attempt

Failure changes the situation: time passes, the trail goes cold, supplies run out, or danger grows. Do not repeat an unchanged roll until it succeeds. A new approach, new evidence, better tools, or more time may justify another attempt.

Success at a cost

When a roll fails by only 1 or 2, the GM may offer success at a cost: you get what you were after, but something else goes wrong. Typical costs are an injury box, broken gear, lost time (tick a clock), a box on a track, or someone noticing. The GM names the cost before you accept; you can always take the plain failure instead. A natural 17 or 18 is never a success at a cost.

Progress clocks

A clock is a circle cut into 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments that tracks something that takes time or builds toward disaster.

  • Danger clocks fill when things go wrong: a failed roll, a success at a cost, or time passing. When one fills, the danger happens.
  • Progress clocks fill as the characters succeed: one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. When one fills, the goal is achieved.
  • The GM sizes the clock (2 = quick, 4 = urgent or simple, 6 = substantial, 8 = long or complex) and shows it to the players.

Investigation and social scenes

Ask concrete questions and explain your approach. Evidence may require several different tasks. A successful roll does not force someone to abandon a core belief or reveal what they do not know.

Discovery clocks and Legend cards

When a mission turns on an unknown subject (a creature, a rival’s power, a strange device), the GM sets up a discovery clock. It fills in three phases, and filling it mints a Legend card: what the characters learned, kept for good. A character’s Legend cards are their collection.

Phase What it means
1. Confront the unknown Effects without a cause. You may suspect, but nothing is confirmed.
2. Identify Name the subject. This is the gate: until its identity is confirmed, what you know about a kind of subject doesn’t yet apply to this one, and your theories stay suspicions. You can still form hypotheses, test them and take ordinary precautions, and whatever is actually true of it works.
3. Uncover Learn how it is beaten or used. Filling this phase fills the clock and earns a Legend card for what you established.
  • Sizing. The GM decides how many segments each phase needs (usually 1–2 to Confront, 2–3 to Identify, 2–3 to Uncover) and which methods can advance it, and shows the clock to the players.
  • Advancing. Each roll that advances the clock uses a concrete method tied to a skill (below): one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. The clock stops at the end of Identify until the subject is confirmed: segments never spill past the gate.
  • The primary investigator. One character leads and owns the Legend card. Others assist under the usual assistance rules, rolling a relevant skill. A character who holds a relevant Legend card can assist with a method they couldn’t otherwise bring (the Legend card is why they’re qualified); it adds nothing beyond the normal assistance.
  • Pushing. A player can push to dig faster or harder: a success advances one extra segment, but a track ticks (below) whatever the roll.

The Legend card

A Legend card is a permanent record of one specific thing the characters have established. It holds only what they found out, never the whole truth: later discoveries add to it with more Legend cards. It grants no bonus to any roll.

  • Weaknesses work anyway. A real weakness works whoever uses it, Legend card or no Legend card. An unfamiliar procedure still needs its method, the knowledge, the skill and the means.
  • What a Legend card is for. It’s what the character knows for certain and can rely on, plan around and teach. A correct deduction, a sensible experiment or an ordinary precaution needs no Legend card.
  • The identification gate. Before a subject is confirmed, you can form hypotheses, test them and take precautions, but what you know about a kind of subject isn’t applied to this one until the Identify phase confirms it. Holding a Legend card never proves that this is that.
  • Shared copies. Everyone who establishes a finding, or is there when a Legend card is earned, gets a shared copy; one character is recorded as its owner. A character who wasn’t there can be taught it later, from a reliable account.
  • What it records. The subject, the precise finding, its limits (what it doesn’t show), the evidence or source, how it was learned, what it cost, the date, its owner and who received it. It’s kept on the character sheet, under Learned in play.

Earning a Legend card

A discovery clock is one way to earn a Legend card. The others need no clock: when a fact is established directly or taught reliably, the GM records the Legend card at once. Information is never free: each source has its price, marked when the Legend card is recorded.

How it was learned What counts What it costs
A discovery clock Filled to the end of Uncover. What the clock cost along the way
Seen or tested firsthand Direct experience, or a sensible experiment. threat clock +1 or a track +1
A reliable account A witness, a record or a book the GM judges reliable. A source’s reputation alone doesn’t make it right, but a reliable account can establish a fact without anyone repeating a dangerous test. a track +1
Its own word, backed up What the subject itself, or someone close to it, tells you, supported by evidence. a track +1
  • A basis for a proposal. An investigation proposal needs a reasonable basis: what the characters have heard, evidence, testimony, their own experience, or a suspicion they can explain. Naming the right answer with nothing behind it doesn’t earn a Legend card. The GM asks what supports an approach, and never rejects it just because it matches the hidden truth.
  • Ideas are free; information isn’t. Proposing a theory costs nothing. Learning something does: a discovery clock’s scenes and failed rolls, or the price of a direct source (above).
  • Enough clues. The GM provides enough clues, and more than one route to each answer: a mission shouldn’t stall on a single missed roll.

The threat clock

A discovery clock always races a threat clock. The setting names the threat clock and what happens when it fills: a creature claiming another victim, a villain escalating, a reactor going critical.

What investigating costs

When an investigation roll fails, or a player pushes, one of the setting’s shared tracks ticks up by one, chosen by how the party is digging. A failed roll can still turn up a lead when the story allows it, but the track still ticks.

Each setting names its Legend cards, their collection, the threat clock, the tracks investigating pushes, each source’s price and the methods. Trails of Blood & Dust: lore cards in a hunter’s almanac; Fracture Lines: data records in an archive; The Shattered Crown: tales in a saga; Aurora: dossiers in a binder.

Chases

Run a chase as a progress clock for the pursuers against a danger clock for the quarry’s escape. Each exchange, the characters roll the skill that moves them (the chase skill, or Athletics on foot): success ticks the pursuers’ clock, failure ticks the escape. Terrain, a tired mount or a damaged engine, and attacks made on the move (at −2) adjust the rolls.

Personal combat

Turn order and actions

Act in descending Agility order. Players choose the order of tied characters; the GM resolves other ties. A round lasts a few seconds. On your turn, move up to your Move (below) and take one meaningful action, such as attacking, reloading, helping, or attempting a task.

Attacking and defending

Roll a ranged skill for a ranged attack, a melee skill for a weapon in hand, or an unarmed skill for fists and grappling. If the attack succeeds, an aware target able to move may defend: Dodge, Parry or Block. A successful defense cancels the hit. Apply injury penalties to every defense.

  • One defense a round. A target gets one defense a round, between its turns, and chooses which when an attack hits.
  • Additional Defense. With this Edge you may attempt a second defense each round at −2, in addition to attack-quality and injury penalties. You cannot defend twice against the same attack.
  • Dodge is Agility −2, with +1 for Trained Athletics or +2 for Athletics as a Specialty or Expert.
  • Parry. Against a weapon in hand or fists, roll a melee skill −3 with a weapon in hand, or an unarmed skill −3 bare-handed against fists. An open hand can’t parry a blade, claws or fangs. Nobody parries a shot or a missile.
  • Block. With a shield, or anything that can stand in for one (a chair, a plank, a crate lid), you can block a weapon in hand, fists, or a thrown or loosed missile: roll a melee skill −2, plus the shield’s bonus. Nothing you can lift blocks a gunshot or a blast. An improvised shield breaks the first time it blocks a hit of 2 or more boxes.
  • Attack quality. A clean hit is harder to escape. When an attack succeeds by 3 or 4, the defense is at −1; by 5 or 6, −2; by 7 or more, −3.
  • Aim. Spend your action aiming at a target you can see: +1 to your next attack against it. An advantage that rewards aiming adds its bonus to this.

Solid cover blocks a shot. Partial cover imposes −2 on the attack. Weapon effects apply after a hit survives any defense. Success margin does not increase damage: a great hit decides what happens instead.

Telling hits

A really good shot, or a blow that lands exactly where it was meant to, lets the attacker say what it does.

  • A telling hit is an attack that succeeds by 5 or more, or on a natural 3 or 4, and isn’t stopped by a defense. The attacker picks one effect below and describes it. The weapon still marks its usual boxes.
  • A called shot. Before rolling, name an effect your weapon can deliver and take −2 on the attack, or −4 for a small target (a hand, an eye, a rope, a lock). If the attack hits at all, the effect happens, whatever the margin. An advantage that rewards aiming cuts this penalty by 1.
  • Both ways. Opponents land telling hits on characters too.

It’s destroyed for good only if it was already vulnerable (a rotten haft, an improvised shield) or the attacker has a weakness to exploit. Armor loses 1 protection until it’s repaired
Open them up Any attack The next attack on this target this round gets +2
Precise Any attack You hit exactly what you aimed at: the lantern, the rope, the tire, the hand on the trigger. It changes the situation, never the rules: no extra boxes, no getting past a defense, no instant death

Limits. A telling hit never adds boxes and never gets past a defense. It never kills outright, disables a character for good, or destroys exceptional equipment: those come only from injury, from the Dying and death rules, or from an established weakness. Only the effects a weapon can deliver are open to it (the With column; a weapon’s entry may add one, as a taser knocks down). Keeping someone’s head down with gunfire isn’t a pin: that’s cover and a threat. The effect still has to make sense: nobody disarms a bear or knocks down a train, and the GM can rule an effect out. Opponents who drop from one hit are already down, so a telling hit against them sets up what comes next, like sending one crashing into another.

Staying alive. A character on the receiving end can shrug off a telling hit’s effect with Hard to put down (once per fight) or by spending a Legend point. Either way, they still take the boxes.

Range

Distances fall into five bands:

Band Distance For example
Close Up to 10 yards Across a room; one move covers it
Short Up to 25 yards Across a street or a courtyard
Medium Up to 100 yards A city block, a long field
Long Up to 300 yards Across a park, a harbor or a small valley
Extreme Up to 1,000 yards, in line of sight Rooftop to rooftop across a town

Every ranged weapon has an effective band, shown in the weapons table. Attacks within it take no penalty. One band beyond it is −2, two bands beyond is −4, and anything farther is out of range. A thrown weapon’s effective band is Close.

Move and reach

Move is how far you go in a turn: 5 yards, +1 for every 2 points of Agility + Body above 20 (rounded down; nothing is taken off below 20), +1 for Trained Athletics or +2 for Athletics as a Specialty or Expert, plus your origin’s speed adjustment, if it has one. Most characters move 6 to 9 yards. Give up your action to run and you cover twice your Move. Threats move 8 yards unless their entry says otherwise.

  • Injury slows you. Wounded (1 box): Move −2 yards. Seriously wounded (2 boxes): half your Move, rounded down, and you can’t run. Incapacitated: you don’t move. Never less than 1 yard while you can act.
  • Hard going halves it. Climbing, swimming, wading, crawling, deep snow or sand, thick brush, rubble or a crowd: half your Move (and half a run), rounded down. Injury and hard going both apply, injury first.
  • Stepping away. You can move away from someone in reach freely; leaving a fight doesn’t give them a free attack.

Mounts and vehicles

A rider or driver moves at the mount’s or vehicle’s Move, not their own, and running it (a gallop, full throttle) takes their action. Attacking from a moving mount or vehicle is −2. A wounded mount slows down under the same injury rule as a person. Starships, aircraft and car chases are too fast for yards: they use range tracks and chase clocks.

Dying and death

  • Dying. A hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes and brings you to three boxes leaves you Incapacitated and Dying. A Dying character dies at the end of the scene unless someone stabilizes them: one action and a successful Medicine roll with a medical kit.
  • A further hit. While you are at three boxes, any hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes kills you. Fists, clubs and knives just keep you down.
  • Certain death. Some fates (a fall into a furnace, a beheading, disintegration) bypass the track entirely.

Your protection is avoiding the hit: a defense (Dodge, Parry or Block), cover, and a Legend point, which reduces a hit by one box.

The battle map

The GM view of the character creator has a battle map for fights where it helps to see who is where. It is a tool, not a rule: everything on it follows the rules in this chapter, and a fight without it works the same way. Open How to use the map on the map itself for the buttons, step by step.

Two kinds of map.

  • A hex map for fights where position matters: cover, doorways, who can reach whom. The GM chooses what one hex is: 1 yard for a brawl in a room, 5 or 10 yards for a street, 50 for open ground. Dragging a token or measuring shows the distance and its range band, so the GM can read the penalty from the Range table above.
  • A range track for fights where only distance matters: a chase, a running fight across open country. It has a lane for the party and one for each range band. Moving a token into the next lane moves it one band in or out.

Tokens. The GM places the party with one button. Their tokens stay linked to the characters in the GM view: marking Injury, Dying, the stunned condition or a Legend point on a token marks it on the character, and the other way round. Opponents come from the setting’s threats with their boxes filled in. Their pips fill as they are hurt, and a token whose boxes are all marked is taken out.

Keeping a map. The map stays in the GM’s browser. Save map to file keeps both maps, their pictures and tokens in one file, to prepare a fight ahead of time or share it with another GM.

The Encounter panel

The GM view also has an Encounter panel that runs a fight by these rules and shows every roll. Add characters from the GM view and opponents from the Threats table (or make one up on the spot), then start: it keeps the turn order by Agility, and for each attack works out the range, the defense (Dodge, Parry or Block, one a round, and a second at −2 with Additional Defense), the boxes marked and any telling hit, offering a Legend point choices at the moment they apply. Hits land on the character cards and the battle map, and with gritty combat on, grievous wounds are rolled and kept. The panel keeps a log and a summary of the fight, which can be saved to a file. It’s a helper, not a rule: the GM can rule any result differently, and the log says so.

The shared table. The GM can start a shared table from the GM view and give the players its code; each player joins from their character’s Play tab. While they’re connected, every roll from a sheet is rolled by the table server, so the whole table sees the same dice as they land, in a live log on the Play tab and on the GM view, along with who rolled, against what, and any telling hit. A point reroll answers its own roll, once. Each player’s character is shared with the GM too: it appears among the GM view’s characters, marked at the table, and its injuries, points, ammunition and form stay in step both ways, whether they’re changed on the sheet, on the GM’s card or in an encounter. In a fight, each player’s Play tab shows the GM’s battle map and the encounter’s turn order and latest results; a player drags their own token, and on their turn sends the GM what they’ll do, which the GM puts into the Encounter panel and runs. The GM can remove a player from the table, and give the table a new code: everyone at it moves with it, and the old code stops working. Without a table, the roller works exactly as it always has.

Making Legend cards

For the GM: when a Legend card is earned, and how to write one. A Legend card is a record of what the characters established, so writing it well matters more than how often you hand one out.

  1. Decide that something is established. A Legend card is earned when a discovery clock fills, or when the characters see or test something themselves, hear it from a source you judge reliable, or are told it by the subject itself with evidence to back it up. A guess, however right, isn’t established, and neither is a rumor. Ask what supports their idea; when the answer is good, let them act on it, and give the Legend card once they’ve confirmed it.
  2. Write the finding narrowly. One fact, in the words the characters would use, and no more than they found out: “bright light drove it back”, not “it fears light”. Two separate facts make two Legend cards. Never write the subject’s whole truth onto a Legend card; later discoveries add more Legend cards.
  3. Write its limits. What the finding doesn’t show: how often it was seen, under what conditions, and what is still unknown. The limits are what keep the next investigation interesting.
  4. Note the evidence or source. What they saw, tested, read or were told, and by whom. A source’s reputation doesn’t make it right: if it was only probably true, the limits say so.
  5. Charge the price. A Legend card from a discovery clock costs nothing more: the scenes, failed rolls and pushes were its price. Any other source has its price (see Earning a Legend card): choose the one that fits how they learned it, and mark it.
  6. Choose the owner and the recipients. Whoever did the finding owns the Legend card. Everyone who was there, or heard it from a reliable source, gets a shared copy.
  7. Record it. Write it on each recipient’s sheet, under Learned in play.

Example: The thing in the mine. Established: Bright light drove it back down the shaft. Limits: Seen once; not whether light harms it, or how long it stays away. Source: What the characters saw at the mine head (seen or tested firsthand). Cost: the threat clock +1 (the night spent watching).

  • When a later finding disagrees. Write a new Legend card that says what they now know; the old one stays on the sheet as what they believed then, and the new one’s limits can say so.
  • A Legend card is never proof of identity. Holding a Legend card about one subject doesn’t prove that the thing in front of them is that subject: the Identify phase does.
  • Weaknesses work anyway. Don’t hold back a real weakness because no one holds the Legend card: if they try it, it works.

Medical care

Diagnosis and stabilization

Roll Medicine when the diagnosis is uncertain or an urgent treatment decision matters. Emergency stabilization takes one action and a successful Medicine roll with a medical kit. It ends Dying and stops deterioration, but removes no boxes.

Field treatment

With a medical kit and several uninterrupted minutes, successful Medicine removes one injury box; success by 5 or more removes two.

Proper care

A proper infirmary grants +2 to Medicine. A successful treatment removes two boxes; success by 5 or more removes three. Allow the time the treatment requires.

Treatment attempts

Each set of injuries permits one field-treatment attempt and one proper-care attempt. Further rolls require a meaningful improvement in care, a new treatment, or recovery time. Simply waiting and trying again is insufficient.

  • A set of injuries is the injury boxes from one encounter or one harmful incident: a fight, a fall, a fire, a night of exposure.
  • Later injuries start a new set. Its attempts can remove only its own boxes. A fresh scratch doesn’t reopen the wounds you already treated: those boxes stay until their own set allows another roll, or rest clears them.
  • What allows another roll: a meaningful improvement (moving from the field to A proper infirmary, a better healer taking over), a new treatment (surgery, a remedy, healing magic), or recovery time (rest that has already cleared a box from that set). The GM decides whether a change is meaningful.
  • Stabilization is separate. Stopping someone Dying is never limited by these attempts and never removes boxes; a treatment roll that removes boxes also stabilizes.

Natural recovery

Rest clears injury one box at a time, and the worse you’re hurt, the longer each box takes. Rest means nothing strenuous: no fighting, no hard travel, no long days of work.

Boxes marked Rest to clear one box
1 A full day
2 At least three days
3 A week, and only in someone’s care

At three boxes, rest alone does nothing until someone has stabilized or treated you; after that, a week of rest in their care clears the first box. A successful treatment is faster. The times add up: from three boxes, rest alone takes about a week and a half to heal completely (a week, then three days, then a day).

Magic

Magic works at two speeds, with a third tool that turns one into the other. Every magical character uses the same three tools; each setting decides who gets which, what they’re called and what they cost.

Tool Speed How it works
Bursts One action A quick spell you know. Roll your magic skill; on a success it happens.
Workings Minutes to nights Anything else. Describe the effect; the GM sets its weight; fill a progress clock.
Charms One action, no roll A finished working stored in an object, released by breaking it.

Bursts

Each burst has a short base effect: one target, for one scene, at Close or Short range. Harm marks 2 boxes; a penalty is −2.

Defense or resistance. A hostile burst says in its description how its target avoids it: a defense or a resistance, never both, and never chosen at the moment of casting.

  • A defense (“Dodge applies”): the burst is an attack, and the casting roll is the attack roll. If it succeeds, the target may defend as against any attack, using their one defense a round, and attack quality applies from the casting margin: succeeded by 3–4, the defense is at −1; by 5–6, −2; by 7 or more, −3.
  • A resistance (“Will resists”, “Body resists”): a contest between the casting roll and the target’s roll on that attribute, by the usual contest rules (a success beats a failure; if both succeed, the larger margin wins; ties go to the target). Attack quality doesn’t apply: the casting margin already counts in the contest. The target’s injury penalty applies only when the resistance is physical (Body).
  • Neither (a burst on an object, or on a willing target): it simply works on a success.

Harm limit. Magic at personal scale marks at most 3 boxes on any one target from one burst, working or charm, whatever its Boosts. Magical harm follows the Injury rules like a weapon’s: a hit of 2 or more boxes that brings someone to three boxes leaves them Dying. Boosts beyond the limit go to the other dimensions: range, duration, or more targets.

Boosts. Your step sets your power (Trained, Specialty, Expert). Each step above the first gives one Boost, chosen as you cast. Each Boost you spend is −1 to the roll, so you can always hold back.

Boost One Boost Two Boosts
Harder +1 box (3 at most), or the penalty becomes −3 +2 boxes (3 at most), or −4
Wider A handful of targets A crowd, or an area
Longer A day Until it’s lifted
Farther One range band farther Two bands farther

The price. A failed burst marks a box of your setting’s price track; a natural 18 marks two, or something answers that shouldn’t.

Workings

  1. Describe the effect. The GM sets its weight and says what it can and can’t do before you start.
  2. Fill the clock with magic skill rolls at your pace (usually ten minutes a roll). Success fills one segment, success by 5 or more two. A failure costs time or ticks a danger clock.
  3. It happens when the clock is full. A working interrupted with energy gathered may go wrong.
Weight Segments Scale
Minor 2 One person, one night, one small thing
Significant 4 A household, a building, a question answered
Major 6 A bloodline, a town, a demon bound

Trappings. The right materials, hour or place remove one segment.

Too slow for a fight. A working takes a minute a roll at the very fastest. To use one in combat, finish it beforehand and store it in a charm.

Charms

Work a working in advance and store it in an object; break it (one action, no roll) to release it. Carry as many ready charms as your magic skill training allows: Trained 2, Specialty 3, Expert 4. Preparation is how slow magic wins a fast fight.

A charm skips the casting roll, not the target’s chance. Whatever a target could dodge or resist when the magic is cast, they can dodge or resist when it comes from a charm. With no casting roll, a charm counts as a success by 0: against a defense, the target defends with no attack-quality penalty; against a resistance, the target resists by succeeding on their roll (a tie goes to the target, as in any contest). A charm never marks more than 3 boxes on one target.

Making your own

The roles and origins in this book are a starting point, not a limit. With the GM’s agreement, any player can create their own role or origin. The creator has a Custom option at the end of each list, and its checklist holds a custom choice to the same rules as everything else.

A custom role

A role is a concept, not a package of powers: it gives no bonus of its own. A custom one only needs a clear place in the group.

  1. Name it and describe it. One sentence for what your character does that no one else does, in the style of the roles above: “Smuggler: knows every back door, bribe and hidden hold.”
  2. Choose a group. Pick the one it belongs with: one of your setting’s groups. The group only sets your sheet’s colors and emblem.
  3. Pick four core skills. The skills it can’t do without. Mark them Trained yourself in step 3 (the built-in roles do this for you); they count toward your normal Trained skills.
  4. Check it with the GM. It should fit the setting and overlap the existing roles as little as possible.

In the creator: in step 1, choose Custom role… at the end of the Role list, then enter its name and group. Write the description in What you bring.

A custom origin

Build it with the GM, one step at a time.

1. The concept

Two or three sentences: where they come from, what sets them apart, and how others see them. It should be something the origins in this book don’t already cover.

2. The tier

The tier measures how much the origin gives on its own. Each tier costs one of your 3 Edge points, so stronger origins start with fewer Edges.

Tier Edges Use it for
0 3 Ordinary people: a homeland, upbringing or culture, with no special abilities
1 2 One small, constant edge: keen senses, a tough hide, a free Trained skill, resistance to one hazard
2 1 Clear inhuman or supernatural gifts: venom, a second form, strong resistances
3 0 Remarkable beings: several strong abilities, and serious weaknesses to match

When in doubt, choose the higher tier.

3. Attribute adjustments

Give adjustments from −2 to +2 per attribute that total zero: a +2 needs a −2 or two −1s. The adjustments move the attribute’s base (+1 starts it at 11) and raise its starting maximum (13 plus the bonus). Most origins change two to four attributes.

An origin that is plainly faster or slower on its feet than a human (long-legged, heavy, built for another gravity) may also take a speed adjustment of +1 or −1 to Move. Most have none. It isn’t an attribute adjustment and needn’t balance.

4. Core rules (tier 1 and up)

One to three short rules that always apply: what they can do (see in the dark, resist cold, a free Trained skill) and what it costs (a weakness, a people’s distrust, a hazard they can’t abide). A strength with no drawback is worth more than it looks, so price it a tier higher.

5. Origin advantages

Write one to three. Each works like a general advantage: +2 to one skill in a narrow situation, or a small, limited permission (“once per scene…”). Each comes with a companion complication: a problem the trait causes, with a clear trigger and a real consequence, which earns a Legend point when it bites, like any complication. A character takes one advantage, general or origin, so make them distinct choices.

6. An alternate form (optional, tier 2 or 3)

If the origin changes shape, give the form attribute bonuses (usually +2 to one attribute and +1 to another), two or three traits, and its drawbacks. A form’s Agility and Body bonuses change its Move, and a fast form may add a speed adjustment of its own. The shared rules for forms in Origins apply. The bonuses count only while the character is in that form: the sheet’s form switch shows both sets of numbers.

In the creator

  1. In step 2, choose Custom origin… at the end of the Origin list.
  2. Name it, choose the tier you agreed, and set its adjustments. The checklist confirms they total zero.
  3. Write its core rules in Other ability (GM approval required) in step 4.
  4. In step 4, choose Custom origin advantage… and enter the advantage and its companion complication.

Example

Hill-folk (tier 1). Stocky herders from high, cold country. Adjustments: Body +1, Presence −1. Core rule: at home in the heights: no penalty for thin air or cold. Origin advantage: Sure-Footed: +2 to Athletics to climb and keep your footing. Companion: Clannish: you trust your own people first; when it matters, accept the cost of that loyalty.

Quick reference

Fine weapon or tool: +2 to one narrow, agreed use of a skill; never with an advantage on the same roll; never to Dodge, Parry or Block.

Creation: attributes start at 10, adjusted by origin (always totaling zero), plus 5 points; maximum 13 plus any origin bonus. 3 points split between origin tier and Edges. Specialist: one Specialty and nine Trained. Expert: one Expert at +5 and seven Trained. One advantage (general or origin) and one complication; an optional Allegiance.

Skills: Untrained = attribute −4; Trained = attribute +1; Specialty = attribute +3; Expert = attribute +5.

Modifiers: favorable +2; ordinary 0; difficult −2; very difficult −4; extreme −6. Natural 3–4 succeeds; natural 17–18 fails.

Assistance: success +1; success by 5 or more +2; 18 causes a complication. One assistant per roll.

Near misses: fail by 1–2 and the GM may offer success at a cost; never on a natural 17–18.

Clocks: 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments. Danger clocks fill on failures and time; progress clocks fill on successes (2 segments on success by 5).

Wounded −2 yd; seriously wounded half, no run. Hard going: half. Stepping away is free. Reach: 1 yd fists, knives, clubs; 2 yd swords, axes; 3 yd spears, polearms.

Clocks

A clock is a circle cut into 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments: 2 for something quick, 4 for urgent or simple, 6 for substantial, 8 for long or complex. A danger clock fills when things go wrong (a failed roll, a success at a cost, time passing), and when it fills the danger happens. A progress clock fills as the characters succeed: one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. A chase is a progress clock for the pursuers racing a danger clock for the quarry's escape.

Anyone can start, tick or remove a clock, so players can tick a progress clock after a success; usually the GM runs them. Ticking or removing a clock needs its full name, though capitals don't matter. A server can run up to 20 clocks at once.

Why Legend Dynamics

Why I Created the Legend Dynamics Roleplaying Game System

I've got a soft spot for 3d6 systems. There's something satisfying about that bell curve, where you have a real feel for your odds before the dice hit the table. And Fate? Fate nailed the storytelling side. I loved how it rewards your character's flaws instead of just punishing them. I kept thinking, why can't I have both?

Legend Dynamics grows out of the 3d6 roll-under tradition of games like GURPS and Hero System, but married to Fate's narrative heart. That combination is the whole point. You roll 3d6 under your skill, and that's the core of it. Your flaws earn you Legend points. A near miss can still succeed, at a cost. A failure moves the story along instead of stopping it cold.

So I built it. The Legend Dynamics RPG puts the rules to work for you. The character creator walks you through each step and checks the math, and the GM tools roll up mission seeds and start the clock. The core rules work in any genre, so whether you're riding a dusty trail or crewing a starship, you're playing the same game. That's the whole idea: less fussing with rules, more time around the table telling the story you came to tell.

Don Smallwood

Legend cards and the price of information

21 October 2026 · Rules change

  • A Legend card records one thing the characters have established: the finding, its limits, the evidence or source, how it was learned, what it cost, and who holds it. Each setting names its cards.
  • A card can be earned by a discovery clock, or directly: seen or tested firsthand, a reliable account, or the subject's own word backed by evidence.
  • Information is never free: each direct source has a price, ticking the threat clock or a track. A clock's card costs what the clock already cost. Proposing an idea is free.
  • A real weakness works whether or not anyone holds a card for it.
  • A new section for the GM: making Legend cards.

Movement, reach and range bands

18 October 2026 · Rules change

  • Tighter range bands: Short 25 yards, Medium 100, Long 300, Extreme 1,000; Close stays 10. Running covers 20 yards.
  • Move: each character has a Move in yards, worked out from their attributes, Athletics and origin; Run is twice Move.
  • Weapon reach: every weapon in hand has a reach of 1, 2 or 3 yards.
  • Injury and hard going slow Move; stepping away from a fight is free.
  • Telling-hit effects depend on the weapon: Pin needs a weapon in hand, fists or a grapple.
  • Origin speed adjustments, and mounts and vehicles.

Balance revision

17 October 2026 · Rules change

  • Fine weapons and tools: +2 to one narrowly defined use of a skill, never a defense.
  • Additional Defense: a second defense each round at −2.
  • Magic limits: Quicker hand, every hostile burst names a defense or a resistance, personal magic marks at most 3 boxes, and charms still face the target's defense.
  • Telling hits: Break gear, Pin, Precise and Knock down clarified, with firm limits.
  • Medical treatment: one field-treatment and one proper-care attempt per set of injuries.
