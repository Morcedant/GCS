                  The Shattered Crown Players' Guide

                  Sword and sorcery for the Legend Dynamics RPG systemBy Don Smallwood

                  A thousand years ago the Crown of Aeon bound a sorcerer-empire together. When the last emperor died, the Crown shattered into nine shards, and the empire with it. Each shard gives power to whoever holds it, and each slowly devours them. Now warlords, sorcerer-princes and merchant-kings fight over the empire’s corpse, and the old things beneath its cities are waking.

                  The Shattered Crown is a sword-and-sorcery setting for the Legend Dynamics RPG system. You are not heroes. You are reavers, thieves, sellswords and sorcerers who live on steel, wits and luck in a world that has no heroes left.

                  The years are counted After the Shattering (AS). The story begins in the year 712 AS, but the GM can set it anywhere in the Crown’s long ruin.

                  The rule to remember

                  Roll 3d6 at or below your adjusted skill to succeed. Your skill already includes your training and attribute. Do not add the attribute again.

                  Example: a skill of 13, with a −2 penalty for bad conditions, gives a target of 11. A roll of 9 succeeds by 2. A roll of 12 fails by 1.

                  How this book is organized

                  This book combines the Legend Dynamics core rules, which every setting shares, with the The Shattered Crown setting: its callings, ancestries, skills, gear, weapons and dangers. In this setting, the core rules’ Legend points are called Fortune points. The material for running the game (venture seeds, the threats, and the GM’s tools) is in a companion book, the GM’s Guide.

                  Create your character

                  Work through the five steps in order. The character creator follows the same steps and checks the rules for you.

                  Step 3: Choose trained skills

                  A Seasoned character chooses ten different skills: one Specialty at its governing attribute +3 and nine Trained skills at attribute +1. A Master chooses one Expert skill at +5 and seven Trained skills at +1. Each Extra Trained skill Edge adds one more Trained skill. All other skills are Untrained at attribute −4 when an attempt is plausible. Each skill has its own final target number; do not add the attribute again when rolling.

                  The Expert’s extra bonus matters most when the work is hard. With an attribute of 13, an Expert has a target of 18, which only natural 17–18 can fail; under a −4 “very difficult” penalty the target is still 14. If Athletics is the Expert skill, Dodge adds +2, the same as a Specialty.

                  Some ancestries give one skill Trained for free; it doesn’t use one of your Trained picks.

                  Broadening. A Master can broaden later (the GM’s call, usually after a significant venture): the Expert skill becomes a Specialty at +3, and two more skills become Trained.

                  Step 4: Describe your character

                  Choose your name, title if any, and a short reason for what you do. Then choose one advantage and one complication.

                  Top 10 reasons

                  1. Gold. Gold, and plenty of it. Everything else follows.
                  2. Vengeance. Someone took everything from me. I mean to take it back, in blood.
                  3. Glory. I want my name in the songs when I’m dust.
                  4. Freedom. No master, no chains, no one to answer to.
                  5. The Crown. Find a shard of the Crown, and the world bends to the one who holds it.
                  6. Knowledge. The old empire’s secrets are out there. I will read them.
                  7. Faith. My god is dying. Someone has to keep the flame.
                  8. Survival. Every day alive is a victory. I mean to have more of them.
                  9. Redemption. I did terrible things. I mean to balance the scales.
                  10. Thrill. Danger is the only thing that makes me feel alive.
                  • Advantage. Choose either a general advantage or one of your ancestry’s advantages. An ancestry advantage replaces the general advantage and always brings its companion complication.
                  • Complication. Choose one complication. You keep it even if you take an ancestry advantage, so a character with an ancestry advantage has two complications.
                  • Allegiance (optional). Choose an allegiance below, or none.

                  An advantage gives +2 to one narrowly defined kind of task when the GM agrees it applies. It cannot improve every use of a broad skill. A complication creates choices and problems; it is not a permanent penalty.

                  Allegiance

                  Anyone can owe allegiance to one of the setting’s powers. It’s a benefit paired with a burden; the burden works like a complication and earns Fortune when it causes you a real problem.

                  The GM can add others met in play (the creator’s GM view has a Campaign allegiances list), and a player can write one with the GM: choose Another allegiance and give it a name, a benefit and a burden.

                  Allegiance Benefit Burden
                  The Thieves’ Guild Once per venture, a fence, a safehouse or a rumor from the streets. Duty: the guild takes its cut, and sends you its jobs.
                  The Iron Company Once per venture, a squad of soldiers, arms or a warhorse. Duty: the company’s contracts come first.
                  The Cult of the Burning God Once per venture, shelter, healing or a handful of zealots. Duty: hunt sorcerers and heretics when the cult demands it.
                  A Sorcerer-Prince Once per venture, gold, a spell worked on your behalf, or a forbidden book. Duty: fetch what the prince desires, and ask no questions.
                  The Merchant Princes Once per venture, passage on a ship or caravan, coin, or an introduction. Duty: protect their trade, and their secrets.
                  A Northern Clan Once per venture, warriors, a longship or a place in the hall. Duty: the clan’s feuds are yours.

                  Step 5: Prepare

                  Your Dodge is Agility −2, plus your Athletics training bonus: +0 if Untrained, +1 if Trained, or +2 if a Specialty or Expert. Begin uninjured, with two Fortune points (three with Extra Fortune) and your gear. Name your friend, or your fine weapon or tool and the one narrow use it improves, if you took those Edges. Describe one connection to another player’s character.

                  Top 10 connections to another character

                  1. Shared a cell. We were chained in the same slave pit and broke out together.
                  2. They saved my life. They pulled me from the sea, or the sands, or a burning city.
                  3. I saved theirs. I cut them down from the gallows, and they haven’t forgotten it.
                  4. Old comrades. We fought in the same company and still watch each other’s backs.
                  5. Rivals. We always want the same treasure, the same title or the same lover.
                  6. Same homeland. We grew up in the same place and know each other’s families.
                  7. An unpaid debt. I owe them money, or a favor, and I haven’t paid it yet.
                  8. A shared secret. We know what really happened in the tomb. No one else does.
                  9. Mentor. They taught me to fight, steal or cast. I’m still learning.
                  10. Bound by an oath. We swore an oath together before a god, or a demon.

                  Untrained actions

                  A plausible untrained attempt uses attribute −4. Specialized work such as surgery requires relevant training or direct guidance. Guidance permits an attempt only when the GM finds it credible; it does not grant the expert’s skill.

                  Gear

                  Gear makes tasks possible rather than easier. Ordinary gear grants no blanket bonus. Missing essential tools can make a task impossible, or very difficult (−4) when the GM finds an attempt plausible.

                  Actions and teamwork

                  When to roll

                  Routine work succeeds automatically when you have appropriate training, gear and time. Roll when failure could change the situation. Before rolling, establish your goal, the relevant skill, any modifiers, and what is at stake.

                  Circumstances Modifier
                  Favorable conditions or excellent preparation +2
                  Ordinary challenge under pressure 0
                  Difficult −2
                  Very difficult −4
                  Extreme −6

                  Apply the main circumstance modifier once. Add separately specified effects such as assistance or injury when they apply. The GM avoids charging twice for the same obstacle.

                  Results and margins

                  Add the three dice. A total at or below the adjusted skill succeeds. Margin of success equals the adjusted skill minus the roll. A larger margin may mean better information, more precise work, or a stronger position.

                  A natural 3 or 4 always succeeds. A natural 17 or 18 always fails. An impossible action receives no roll.

                  In an opposed contest, each side rolls its relevant skill. One success beats one failure. If both succeed, the larger success margin wins. A tie, or two failures, favors the defender or preserves the situation. For an automatic success above its target, count its contest margin as zero.

                  Assistance

                  One qualified assistant can roll a relevant skill before the lead character rolls. Success grants +1 to the lead roll; success by 5 or more grants +2. Failure gives no bonus. An 18 introduces a complication described by the GM. Other characters can do other tasks but cannot stack assistance bonuses on the same roll.

                  Failure and another attempt

                  Failure changes the situation: time passes, the trail goes cold, supplies run out, or danger grows. Do not repeat an unchanged roll until it succeeds. A new approach, new evidence, better tools, or more time may justify another attempt.

                  Success at a cost

                  When a roll fails by only 1 or 2, the GM may offer success at a cost: you get what you were after, but something else goes wrong. Typical costs are an injury box, broken gear, lost time (tick a clock), a box on a track, or someone noticing. The GM names the cost before you accept; you can always take the plain failure instead. A natural 17 or 18 is never a success at a cost.

                  Progress clocks

                  A clock is a circle cut into 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments that tracks something that takes time or builds toward disaster.

                  • Danger clocks fill when things go wrong: a failed roll, a success at a cost, or time passing. When one fills, the danger happens.
                  • Progress clocks fill as the characters succeed: one segment for a success, two for success by 5 or more. When one fills, the goal is achieved.
                  • The GM sizes the clock (2 = quick, 4 = urgent or simple, 6 = substantial, 8 = long or complex) and shows it to the players.

                  Investigation and social scenes

                  Ask concrete questions and explain your approach. Evidence may require several different tasks. A successful roll does not force someone to abandon a core belief or reveal what they do not know.

                  Chases

                  Run a chase as a progress clock for the pursuers against a danger clock for the quarry’s escape. Each exchange, the characters roll the skill that moves them (Riding, or Athletics on foot): success ticks the pursuers’ clock, failure ticks the escape. Terrain, a tired mount or a damaged engine, and attacks made on the move (at −2) adjust the rolls.

                  Personal combat

                  Turn order and actions

                  Act in descending Agility order. Players choose the order of tied characters; the GM resolves other ties. A round lasts a few seconds. On your turn, move a short distance (up to 10 yards: the Close band below) and take one meaningful action, such as attacking, reloading, helping, or attempting a task.

                  Attacking and defending

                  Roll Marksmanship for a ranged attack, Melee for a weapon in hand, or Brawling for fists and grappling. If the attack succeeds, an aware target able to move may defend: Dodge, Parry or Block. A successful defense cancels the hit. Apply injury penalties to every defense.

                  • One defense a round. A target gets one defense a round, between its turns, and chooses which when an attack hits.
                  • Additional Defense. With this Edge you may attempt a second defense each round at −2, in addition to attack-quality and injury penalties. You cannot defend twice against the same attack.
                  • Dodge is Agility −2, with +1 for Trained Athletics or +2 for Athletics as a Specialty or Expert.
                  • Parry. Against a weapon in hand or fists, roll Melee −3 with a weapon in hand, or Brawling −3 bare-handed against fists. An open hand can’t parry a blade, claws or fangs. Nobody parries a shot or a missile.
                  • Block. With a shield, or anything that can stand in for one (a chair, a plank, a crate lid), you can block a weapon in hand, fists, or a thrown or loosed missile: roll Melee −2, plus the shield’s bonus. Nothing you can lift blocks a gunshot or a blast. An improvised shield breaks the first time it blocks a hit of 2 or more boxes.
                  • Attack quality. A clean hit is harder to escape. When an attack succeeds by 3 or 4, the defense is at −1; by 5 or 6, −2; by 7 or more, −3.
                  • Aim. Spend your action aiming at a target you can see: +1 to your next attack against it. An advantage that rewards aiming adds its bonus to this.

                  Solid cover blocks a shot. Partial cover imposes −2 on the attack. Weapon effects apply after a hit survives any defense. Success margin does not increase damage: a great hit decides what happens instead.

                  Telling hits

                  A really good shot, or a blow that lands exactly where it was meant to, lets the attacker say what it does.

                  • A telling hit is an attack that succeeds by 5 or more, or on a natural 3 or 4, and isn’t stopped by a defense. The attacker picks one effect below and describes it. The weapon still marks its usual boxes.
                  • A called shot. Before rolling, name the effect you want and take −2 on the attack, or −4 for a small target (a hand, an eye, a rope, a lock). If the attack hits at all, the effect happens, whatever the margin. An advantage that rewards aiming cuts this penalty by 1.
                  • Both ways. Opponents land telling hits on characters too.
                  Effect What happens
                  Disarm The weapon or held item drops, or flies a few yards away
                  Knock down The target falls prone, or is driven back a few steps. Standing up uses the target’s move on their next turn; they still take their action
                  Pin The target is held, tangled or pinned down. Their next action goes to getting free, and then they are free: no roll needed. Keeping them pinned takes another successful attack
                  Rattle −2 to the target’s next roll
                  Break gear A weapon, shield or tool is knocked out of use (jammed, bent, unstrung, cracked) until its owner spends an action putting it right. It’s destroyed for good only if it was already vulnerable (a rotten haft, an improvised shield) or the attacker has a weakness to exploit. Armor loses 1 protection until it’s repaired
                  Open them up The next attack on this target this round gets +2
                  Precise You hit exactly what you aimed at: the lantern, the rope, the tire, the hand on the trigger. It changes the situation, never the rules: no extra boxes, no getting past a defense, no instant death

                  Limits. A telling hit never adds boxes and never gets past a defense. It never kills outright, disables a character for good, or destroys exceptional equipment: those come only from injury, from the Dying and death rules, or from an established weakness. The effect has to make sense: nobody disarms a bear or knocks down a train, and the GM can rule an effect out. Opponents who drop from one hit are already down, so a telling hit against them sets up what comes next, like sending one crashing into another.

                  Staying alive. A character on the receiving end can shrug off a telling hit’s effect with Hard to put down (once per fight) or by spending a Fortune point. Either way, they still take the boxes.

                  Range

                  Distances fall into five bands:

                  Band Distance For example
                  Close Up to 10 yards Across a room; one move covers it
                  Short Up to 25 yards Across a street or a courtyard
                  Medium Up to 100 yards A city block, a long field
                  Long Up to 300 yards Across a park, a harbor or a small valley
                  Extreme Up to 1,000 yards, in line of sight Rooftop to rooftop across a town

                  Every ranged weapon has an effective band, shown in the weapons table. Attacks within it take no penalty. One band beyond it is −2, two bands beyond is −4, and anything farther is out of range. Weapons in hand and fists reach only someone next to you; move into reach as part of your turn. A thrown weapon’s effective band is Close.

                  Moving: your move each turn covers the Close band. Give up your action to run and you cover twice that, 20 yards.

                  Injury

                  Boxes marked Condition Effect
                  0 Uninjured No injury penalty
                  1 Wounded −1 to physical actions
                  2 Seriously wounded −2 to physical actions
                  3 Incapacitated Unable to act; medical aid needed

                  Injury penalties replace each other rather than stacking. They apply to movement, attacks, Dodge, and other tasks the injury physically hinders.

                  Dying and death

                  • Dying. A hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes and brings you to three boxes leaves you Incapacitated and Dying. A Dying character dies at the end of the scene unless someone stabilizes them: one action and a successful Medicine roll with a healer’s satchel.
                  • A further hit. While you are at three boxes, any hit from a weapon that marks 2 or more boxes kills you. Fists, clubs and knives just keep you down.
                  • Certain death. Some fates (a fall into a furnace, a beheading, disintegration) bypass the track entirely.

                  Your protection is avoiding the hit: a defense (Dodge, Parry or Block), cover, and a Fortune point, which reduces a hit by one box.

                  Optional rule: gritty combat

                  For a harder, bloodier game, the GM can turn on gritty combat for the campaign (the creator has a switch on the Condition card and in the GM view). The rest of the rules stay the same.

                  Grievous hits. A hit that marks 2 or more boxes is grievous when the attack succeeded by 7 or more, rolled a natural 3 or 4, or left the target Dying.

                  • A character rolls 3d6 on the table below and suffers the result on top of the boxes.
                  • An opponent is simply taken out: no roll, no boxes to count. Describe how, or let the GM view roll a description.
                  • With a telling hit, the attacker also chooses which limb.

                  Medical care

                  Healers have herbs, needles, wine and prayers. Poison and fever kill more adventurers than swords do. The Mend cantrip clears one box once per set of injuries; it does not replace a healer’s care.

                  Diagnosis and stabilization

                  Roll Medicine when the diagnosis is uncertain or an urgent treatment decision matters. Emergency stabilization takes one action and a successful Medicine roll with a healer’s satchel. It ends Dying and stops deterioration, but removes no boxes.

                  Field treatment

                  With a healer’s satchel and several uninterrupted minutes, successful Medicine removes one injury box; success by 5 or more removes two.

                  Proper care

                  A temple infirmary or a master healer’s house grants +2 to Medicine. A successful treatment removes two boxes; success by 5 or more removes three. Allow the time the treatment requires.

                  Treatment attempts

                  Each set of injuries permits one field-treatment attempt and one proper-care attempt. Further rolls require a meaningful improvement in care, a new treatment, or recovery time. Simply waiting and trying again is insufficient.

                  • A set of injuries is the injury boxes from one encounter or one harmful incident: a fight, a fall, a fire, a night of exposure.
                  • Later injuries start a new set. Its attempts can remove only its own boxes. A fresh scratch doesn’t reopen the wounds you already treated: those boxes stay until their own set allows another roll, or rest clears them.
                  • What allows another roll: a meaningful improvement (moving from the field to A temple infirmary or a master healer’s house, a better healer taking over), a new treatment (surgery, a remedy, healing magic), or recovery time (rest that has already cleared a box from that set). The GM decides whether a change is meaningful.
                  • Stabilization is separate. Stopping someone Dying is never limited by these attempts and never removes boxes; a treatment roll that removes boxes also stabilizes.

                  Natural recovery

                  Rest clears injury one box at a time, and the worse you’re hurt, the longer each box takes. Rest means nothing strenuous: no fighting, no hard travel, no long days of work.

                  Boxes marked Rest to clear one box
                  1 A full day
                  2 At least three days
                  3 A week, and only in someone’s care

                  At three boxes, rest alone does nothing until someone has stabilized or treated you; after that, a week of rest in their care clears the first box. A successful treatment is faster. The times add up: from three boxes, rest alone takes about a week and a half to heal completely (a week, then three days, then a day).

                  Magic

                  Magic works at two speeds, with a third tool that turns one into the other. Every magical character uses the same three tools; this setting decides who gets which, what they’re called and what they cost.

                  Tool Speed How it works
                  Cantrips One action A quick spell you know. Roll Sorcery; on a success it happens.
                  Rituals Minutes to nights Anything else. Describe the effect; the GM sets its weight; fill a progress clock.
                  Talismans One action, no roll A finished ritual stored in an object, released by breaking it.

                  Cantrips

                  Each cantrip has a short base effect: one target, for one scene, at Close or Short range. Harm marks 2 boxes; a penalty is −2.

                  Defense or resistance. A hostile cantrip says in its description how its target avoids it: a defense or a resistance, never both, and never chosen at the moment of casting.

                  • A defense (“Dodge applies”): the cantrip is an attack, and the casting roll is the attack roll. If it succeeds, the target may defend as against any attack, using their one defense a round, and attack quality applies from the casting margin: succeeded by 3–4, the defense is at −1; by 5–6, −2; by 7 or more, −3.
                  • A resistance (“Will resists”, “Body resists”): a contest between the casting roll and the target’s roll on that attribute, by the usual contest rules (a success beats a failure; if both succeed, the larger margin wins; ties go to the target). Attack quality doesn’t apply: the casting margin already counts in the contest. The target’s injury penalty applies only when the resistance is physical (Body).
                  • Neither (a cantrip on an object, or on a willing target): it simply works on a success.

                  Harm limit. Magic at personal scale marks at most 3 boxes on any one target from one cantrip, ritual or talisman, whatever its Boosts. Magical harm follows the Injury rules like a weapon’s: a hit of 2 or more boxes that brings someone to three boxes leaves them Dying. Boosts beyond the limit go to the other dimensions: range, duration, or more targets.

                  Boosts. Your Seal sets your power (the First Seal at Trained, the Second at Specialty, the Third at Expert). Each Seal above the first gives one Boost, chosen as you cast. Each Boost you spend is −1 to the roll, so you can always hold back.

                  Boost One Boost Two Boosts
                  Harder +1 box (3 at most), or the penalty becomes −3 +2 boxes (3 at most), or −4
                  Wider A handful of targets A crowd, or an area
                  Longer A day Until it’s lifted
                  Farther One range band farther Two bands farther

                  The price. A failed cantrip marks a box of Corruption; a natural 18 marks two, or something answers that shouldn’t.

                  Rituals

                  1. Describe the effect. The GM sets its weight and says what it can and can’t do before you start.
                  2. Fill the clock with Sorcery rolls at your pace (usually ten minutes a roll). Success fills one segment, success by 5 or more two. A failure costs time or ticks a danger clock.
                  3. It happens when the clock is full. A ritual interrupted with energy gathered may go wrong.
                  Weight Segments Scale
                  Minor 2 One person, one night, one small thing
                  Significant 4 A household, a building, a question answered
                  Major 6 A bloodline, a town, a demon bound

                  Trappings. The right materials, hour or place remove one segment.

                  Too slow for a fight. A ritual takes a minute a roll at the very fastest. To use one in combat, finish it beforehand and store it in a talisman.

                  Talismans

                  Work a ritual in advance and store it in an object; break it (one action, no roll) to release it. Carry as many ready talismans as your Sorcery training allows: Trained 2, Specialty 3, Expert 4. Preparation is how slow magic wins a fast fight.

                  A talisman skips the casting roll, not the target’s chance. Whatever a target could dodge or resist when the magic is cast, they can dodge or resist when it comes from a talisman. With no casting roll, a talisman counts as a success by 0: against a defense, the target defends with no attack-quality penalty; against a resistance, the target resists by succeeding on their roll (a tie goes to the target, as in any contest). A talisman never marks more than 3 boxes on one target.

                  Troubles

                  The party shares four Troubles. Infamy is the rulers and the watch taking notice; Doom is the old powers learning your names; Debt is what you owe moneylenders, patrons and sorcerers; Hunger is supplies, coin and morale running thin on the road. They fill as your deeds catch up with you; the GM narrates what each box means.

                  • Marking: killing in the open or a beast form seen in town (Infamy); blatant sorcery or taking a cursed treasure (Doom); borrowing or buying on credit (Debt); each day in the wilds without supplies (Hunger). A success at a cost can mark any of them.
                  • Clearing: lying low between ventures (Infamy); a cleansing rite (Doom); paying up (Debt); reaching a town, or a Survival roll each day in the wilds (Hunger).

                  Running ventures

                  The chronicle

                  End each venture with a short entry, written together:

                  1. The venture. What happened, and did we succeed: fully, partly, or not at all?
                  2. Who stood out? A moment worth remembering.
                  3. Complications. Which ones came into play, and what did they cost?
                  4. What changed? New allies or enemies, damage, open threads for next time.
                  5. Advancement. After a significant venture, each character earns 1 advancement point; at a milestone, 2 more.

                  Sorcery

                  Sorcery is real, rare and expensive. Everyone else can resist it, fear it and pay for it.

                  The world

                  The empire is gone, but its bones are everywhere: roads no one repairs, aqueducts that feed dead cities, sorcerer-towers that still glow at night. Its heirs quarrel over the pieces.

                  • The Gilded Cities. The empire’s heart, now a coast of rival city-states: silk, lotus, slave markets and assassins. Ruled by merchant princes and the occasional sorcerer-prince.
                  • The Frozen North. Clans, longships and giants’ graves. Northlanders raid south every summer and trade every winter.
                  • The Burning Sands. Nomads, caravan cities and buried temples of the serpent kings who ruled before humans.
                  • The Shattered Isles. Corsair havens, sea-witches and sunken ruins; the Islanders’ free captains answer to no one.
                  • The Deeps. Deepkin cities beneath the mountains, and below them, older things that dream.
                  • The Ashlands. The empire’s blasted center, where the Crown broke. Nothing grows; shards and madmen are found there.

                  Powers

                  • The Crown shards. Nine in all; four are held by known rulers, three are lost, and two are rumors. Each grants one great power and whispers to its bearer.
                  • The sorcerer-princes. Rulers who bought their thrones with sorcery and keep them with fear.
                  • The merchant princes. Gold rules the Gilded Cities more than any king.
                  • The Cult of the Burning God. Zealots who burn sorcerers, and sometimes their neighbors.
                  • The Iron Company and its rivals. Mercenary companies who decide most wars, for a price.
                  • The Thieves’ Guild. Every city has one; they don’t all get along.

                  Making your own

                  The callings and ancestries in this book are a starting point, not a limit. With the GM’s agreement, any player can create their own calling or ancestry. The creator has a Custom option at the end of each list, and its checklist holds a custom choice to the same rules as everything else.

                  A custom calling

                  A calling is a concept, not a package of powers: it gives no bonus of its own. A custom one only needs a clear place in the group.

                  1. Name it and describe it. One sentence for what your character does that no one else does, in the style of the callings above: “Gladiator: fought for the crowd in the arena pits, and still knows how to win one.”
                  2. Choose a group. Pick the one it belongs with: Steel, Shadow or Lore. The group only sets your sheet’s colors and emblem.
                  3. Pick four core skills. The skills it can’t do without. Mark them Trained yourself in step 3 (the built-in callings do this for you); they count toward your normal Trained skills.
                  4. Check it with the GM. It should fit the setting and overlap the existing callings as little as possible.

                  In the creator: in step 1, choose Custom calling… at the end of the Calling list, then enter its name and group. Write the description in What you bring.

                  A custom ancestry

                  Build it with the GM, one step at a time.

                  1. The concept

                  Two or three sentences: where they come from, what sets them apart, and how others see them. It should be something the ancestries in this book don’t already cover.

                  2. The tier

                  The tier measures how much the ancestry gives on its own. Each tier costs one of your 3 Edge points, so stronger ancestries start with fewer Edges.

                  Tier Edges Use it for
                  0 3 Ordinary people: a homeland, upbringing or culture, with no special abilities
                  1 2 One small, constant edge: keen senses, a tough hide, a free Trained skill, resistance to one hazard
                  2 1 Clear inhuman or supernatural gifts: venom, a second form, strong resistances
                  3 0 Remarkable beings: several strong abilities, and serious weaknesses to match

                  When in doubt, choose the higher tier.

                  3. Attribute adjustments

                  Give adjustments from −2 to +2 per attribute that total zero: a +2 needs a −2 or two −1s. The adjustments move the attribute’s base (+1 starts it at 11) and raise its starting maximum (13 plus the bonus). Most ancestries change two to four attributes.

                  4. Core rules (tier 1 and up)

                  One to three short rules that always apply: what they can do (see in the dark, resist cold, a free Trained skill) and what it costs (a weakness, a people’s distrust, a hazard they can’t abide). A strength with no drawback is worth more than it looks, so price it a tier higher.

                  5. Ancestry advantages

                  Write one to three. Each works like a general advantage: +2 to one skill in a narrow situation, or a small, limited permission (“once per scene…”). Each comes with a companion complication: a problem the trait causes, with a clear trigger and a real consequence, which earns a Fortune point when it bites, like any complication. A character takes one advantage, general or ancestry, so make them distinct choices.

                  6. An alternate form (optional, tier 2 or 3)

                  If the ancestry changes shape, give the form attribute bonuses (usually +2 to one attribute and +1 to another), two or three traits, and its drawbacks. The shared rules for forms in Ancestries apply. The bonuses count only while the character is in that form: the sheet’s form switch shows both sets of numbers.

                  In the creator

                  1. In step 2, choose Custom ancestry… at the end of the Ancestry list.
                  2. Name it, choose the tier you agreed, and set its adjustments. The checklist confirms they total zero.
                  3. Write its core rules in Other ability (GM approval required) in step 4.
                  4. In step 4, choose Custom ancestry advantage… and enter the advantage and its companion complication.

                  Quick reference

                  Fine weapon or tool: +2 to one narrow, agreed use of a skill; never with an advantage on the same roll; never to Dodge, Parry or Block.

                  Creation: attributes start at 10, adjusted by ancestry (always totaling zero), plus 5 points; maximum 13 plus any ancestry bonus. 3 points split between ancestry tier and Edges. Seasoned: one Specialty and nine Trained. Master: one Expert at +5 and seven Trained. One advantage (general or ancestry) and one complication; an optional Allegiance.

                  Skills: Untrained = attribute −4; Trained = attribute +1; Specialty = attribute +3; Expert (Master) = attribute +5.

                  Modifiers: favorable +2; ordinary 0; difficult −2; very difficult −4; extreme −6. Natural 3–4 succeeds; natural 17–18 fails.

                  Assistance: success +1; success by 5 or more +2; 18 causes a complication. One assistant per roll.

                  Near misses: fail by 1–2 and the GM may offer success at a cost; never on a natural 17–18.

                  Clocks: 2, 4, 6 or 8 segments. Danger clocks fill on failures and time; progress clocks fill on successes (2 segments on success by 5).

                  Combat: move plus one action. Hit → one defense a round (Dodge, Parry or Block; −1 to −3 against a clean hit; Additional Defense: a second, at −2) → weapon effect. Dodge = Agility −2 plus Athletics training (+0, +1, +2). Parry −3, Block −2 plus the shield. Aim: +1. Telling hit (by 5+ or natural 3–4): the attacker picks an effect, which never adds boxes or kills outright; called shot −2 (small target −4). A 2-box weapon that brings you to three boxes leaves you Dying; at three boxes, another 2-box hit kills.

                  Medicine: stabilize in one action. Field care clears 1 box (2 on margin 5+). Proper care gives +2 and clears 2 (3 on margin 5+). Each set of injuries (one fight or incident) gets one field and one proper-care attempt; more need better care, a new treatment or recovery time. Rest clears a box in a day at 1 box, three days at 2, a week at 3 (in someone’s care).

                  Fortune: start each session with two. Reroll, reduce an injury by one box, shrug off a telling hit, or establish a small preparation. Earn one from an accepted complication.

                  The GM’s tools

                  The battle map

                  The GM view of the character creator has a battle map for fights where it helps to see who is where. It is a tool, not a rule: everything on it follows the Personal combat rules in the Players’ Guide, and a fight without it works the same way. Open How to use the map on the map itself for the buttons, step by step.

                  Two kinds of map.

                  • A hex map for fights where position matters: cover, doorways, who can reach whom. The GM chooses what one hex is: 1 yard for a brawl in a room, 5 or 10 yards for a street, 50 for open ground. Dragging a token or measuring shows the distance and its range band, so the GM can read the penalty from the Range table in the Players’ Guide.
                  • A range track for fights where only distance matters: a chase, a running fight across open country. It has a lane for the party and one for each range band. Moving a token into the next lane moves it one band in or out.

                  Tokens. The GM places the party with one button. Their tokens stay linked to the characters in the GM view: marking Injury, Dying, the stunned condition or a Fortune point on a token marks it on the character, and the other way round. Opponents come from the setting’s threats with their boxes filled in. Their pips fill as they are hurt, and a token whose boxes are all marked is taken out.

                  Keeping a map. The map stays in the GM’s browser. Save map to file keeps both maps, their pictures and tokens in one file, to prepare a fight ahead of time or share it with another GM.

                  The Encounter panel

                  The GM view also has an Encounter panel that runs a fight by the Personal combat rules and shows every roll. Add characters from the GM view and opponents from the Threats table (or make one up on the spot), then start: it keeps the turn order by Agility, and for each attack works out the range, the defense (Dodge, Parry or Block, one a round, and a second at −2 with Additional Defense), the boxes marked and any telling hit, offering a Fortune point choices at the moment they apply. Hits land on the character cards and the battle map, and with gritty combat on, grievous wounds are rolled and kept. The panel keeps a log and a summary of the fight, which can be saved to a file. It’s a helper, not a rule: the GM can rule any result differently, and the log says so. For each attack, choose what it’s made of (cold iron, silver or fire), and the panel applies the rules that turn on it: a shapechanger’s Tough in their form (and what gets past it), a weakness that marks one box more, and a creature only the right weapon can hurt.

                      Quick reference

                      Resolution
                      3d6 at or below adjusted skill. Natural 3–4 always succeeds; 17–18 always fails. Margin = target − roll.
                      Opposed
                      Success beats failure; both succeed → larger margin wins; ties and double failures favor the defender.
                      Combat
                      Move a short distance and take one action. Hit → target may defend once per round: Dodge, Parry or Block → weapon effect. Partial cover −2.
                      Defending
                      One defense a round; Additional Defense: a second, at −2, never twice against one attack. Dodge: Agility −2 + Athletics. Parry a weapon in hand or fists: Melee or Brawling −3. Block a weapon in hand, fists or a missile with a shield or anything that stands in for one: Melee −2 + the shield's bonus (never a gunshot or blast); an improvised one breaks on a 2+ box hit. Attack succeeded by 3–4: defense −1; 5–6: −2; 7+: −3. Aim (one action): +1 to your next attack on that target.
                      Telling hit
                      Hit by 5+ or a natural 3–4, not defended: the attacker picks one effect (disarm; knock down: standing uses their move; pin: their next action frees them; rattle −2; break gear: out of use until an action fixes it; open them up +2; precise). Called shot: −2 (small target −4), and any hit gets the effect. No extra boxes, never an outright kill. Shrug it off: Hard to put down (once a fight) or a point.
                      Range
                      Close 10 yd, Short 25 yd, Medium 100 yd, Long 300 yd, Extreme 1,000 yd. Within a weapon's effective band: no penalty; one band beyond −2, two −4, farther out of range. Move: 10 yd; run (no action): 20 yd.
                      Dying
                      A 2-box weapon that brings you to 3 boxes: Dying; dead at the end of the scene unless stabilized (one action, Medicine, healer's satchel). At 3 boxes, another 2-box hit kills.
                      Medicine
                      Stabilize: one action. Field care clears 1 box (2 on margin 5+). Proper care gives +2, clears 2 (3 on margin 5+). One field and one proper-care attempt per set of injuries (one fight or incident); more need better care, a new treatment or recovery time.
                      Rest
                      Clearing one box: a full day at 1 box, at least three days at 2, a week at 3 (and only once someone has stabilized or treated you).
                      Fortune
                      Reroll (keep second), reduce an injury by one box, shrug off a telling hit, or establish a small preparation.
                      Damage
                      Fists, daggers, slings: 1 box. Swords, axes, bows, crossbows, pistols, claws: 2. Arquebus: 3 and Dying. Greatweapon hit by 5+: Dying.
                      Missiles
                      Quiver 20, bolts 12, pistol and arquebus 1 shot. Crossbow reload: an action; firearms: a full round. Natural 18: string snaps or powder fails.
                      Sorcery
                      Cantrips: an action and a Sorcery roll; known Trained 2, Specialty 3, Expert 4. Seals: Specialty 1 Boost, Expert 2 (−1 each to the roll). Rituals: 2-, 4- or 6-segment clock. Talismans: 2, 3, 4. Enchantments: a ritual worked into an item, +1 segment to last a day, +2 a moon; they share the talisman limit.
                      Corruption
                      Failed cast: 1 box; natural 18: 2; finishing a Major ritual: 1. Marked, Tainted, Twisted, Lost. A cleansing rite or pilgrimage clears one.
